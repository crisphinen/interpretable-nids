\section{The \texorpdfstring{$\alpha$}{alpha}-Gate and Interpretability Trade-Off}
\label{sec:alpha}

We sweep $\lambda_\alpha$ over six values, five seeds each
(Table~\ref{tab:alpha}). The regulariser raises the learned gate \emph{monotonically}.
On CTU-IoT-23, $\alpha$ rises from $0.38$ ($\lambda_\alpha{=}0$) to $0.89$
($\lambda_\alpha{=}1.0$); on CIC-IoT-2023, from $0.32$ to $0.95$. F1 does not move:
the known-class \emph{validation} F1 (the model-selection split reported in this sweep)
stays near $0.933$ on CTU and $0.818$--$0.819$ on CIC; the corresponding
held-out \emph{test} F1 is $0.933$ on CTU (Table~\ref{M-tab:main}). OOD AUROC is flat on CTU
(within $0.016$ across the sweep) and rises on CIC, from $0.657$ at
$\lambda_\alpha{=}0$ to $0.675$--$0.691$ for every $\lambda_\alpha{\ge}0.05$, a
gain of $0.02$--$0.03$ (about one seed std) that accompanies the increase in $\alpha$; as the
next paragraph shows, it is not evidence that the decision moved onto the rules.

\textbf{Does raising $\alpha$ actually move the decision onto the rules? No.} A larger
gate weight need not mean more rule reliance, because the two heads can emit logits of
different magnitudes and the learned weights can compensate. Testing reliance directly by
branch agreement (Table~\ref{tab:headagree}), the deployed prediction stays with the
\emph{neural-only} head at every $\lambda_\alpha$, and its agreement with the
\emph{rule-only} head rises only mildly as $\alpha$ increases (CTU $32\%$ to $51\%$),
staying well below neural agreement.
So $\lambda_\alpha$ tunes the gate scalar but not decision-level reliance on the rules;
we describe $\alpha$ as the learned rule-head weight in the logit blend, not as a measure
of how interpretable the decision is. (This is also a global scalar gate, so it cannot route
individual hard flows to the neural branch.) Each point was trained from scratch.

\section{Concept Leakage and the \texorpdfstring{$\gamma$}{gamma} Ablation}
\label{sec:leakage}

Table~\ref{tab:cbm_concepts} reports mean concept prediction accuracy for
each CBM variant over five seeds. This quantity is the \emph{concept accuracy} of the main text: agreement between the bottleneck's predicted concept and
the declared threshold label, i.e.\ how faithfully the model reproduces the
declared predicate, not whether the predicate is semantically ``correct''. JointCBM without concept regularisation
($\gamma{=}0$) reaches 87.4\% on CTU and 96.3\% on CIC. On CTU, accuracy rises
to 94.2\% at $\gamma{=}1.0$ (not monotonically: 89.1\% at $\gamma{=}0.1$, 88.9\% at $0.5$); at $\gamma{=}0$ the weakest concept
is \texttt{is\_persistent} at 81\% (it rises to 91\% by $\gamma{=}1.0$). On CIC it rises from 96.3\% to 97.2\%, with
every concept above 90\%. SequentialCBM reaches near-perfect concept accuracy
(99.9\% / 99.5\%) because its concept head is trained on the concept labels
alone and frozen before the classifier is trained. HybridCBM is similar
(99.6\% / 99.2\%): its skip connection lets the classifier bypass the
bottleneck, so the concept head is under less classification pressure and can
fit the concept labels more closely. Higher concept accuracy means closer agreement
with the threshold labels; as the OOD results show, it does not by itself buy
better detection.

Higher agreement with the labels does not by itself show that the bottleneck is free of
\emph{concept leakage}. We therefore train a linear classifier on the
training split to predict the class from (i) the soft predicted concepts
$\hat{\mathbf{c}}\in[0,1]^K$ and (ii) the same predictions thresholded at $0.5$, and report
the test-accuracy difference. This difference measures the additional predictive utility
of the continuous values for these probes. It is small on CTU-IoT-23 for $\gamma{\le}0.5$
(five-seed means $0.001$--$0.013$) but reaches $0.084{\pm}.088$ at $\gamma{=}1.0$, where two of five
seeds gain $0.17$--$0.21$; on CIC-IoT-2023 it is $0.04$--$0.06$ and grows rather than shrinks with
$\gamma$ ($\gamma{=}0$: $0.041{\pm}.008$; $\gamma{=}1.0$: $0.058{\pm}.008$).

A small difference does not establish semantic fidelity. Both probes use \emph{predicted}
concepts, and the thresholded predictions are not the declared predicates: where they
disagree with the declared labels, the pattern of disagreement can itself carry class
information. The predicate-replacement test of Section~\ref{sec:concept_intervention}
therefore uses the declared predicates directly.

\section{Temporal (Timestamp-Ordered) Split on CTU-IoT-23}
\label{sec:temporal}

Random flow partitioning can leak device or session identity between train and test. A
strict device- or session-disjoint split is harder for the CTU attacks, which come from
very few source hosts (Okiru: 4, DDoS: 7, C\&C-HeartBeat: 5 source IPs), so we instead
build the known-class splits in time: we take the same per-class sample as the main
split, order the flows of each known class by capture timestamp (timestamps are compared
only within a class), and assign that class's earliest 70\% to training, the next 15\% to
validation and the latest 15\% to test (five seeds; within each class, test strictly
follows train; the held-out unknown families are unchanged). Timestamp ordering does not by itself guarantee a session- or
capture-disjoint split, individual sessions or captures may straddle the train/test
boundary, so we claim temporal ordering, not capture-, session- or device-disjointness;
the small number of source hosts makes a strict capture-disjoint split harder but we do not
claim it impossible, and it remains untested. This protocol probes a property of the CTU-IoT-23 labelling:
the \emph{DDoS} family is temporally bimodal, a high-rate flood capture (September 2018,
median Rate ${\approx}9.3{\times}10^{5}$\,pps) and near-zero-rate captures (December 2018
onward, median Rate ${<}1$), so a timestamp split can train on one DDoS regime and test on the
other. This makes \emph{every} model score F1 $0.00$ on DDoS on every seed; with the main split's
31-feature list on the same sample only two JointCBM $\gamma{=}0.5$ seeds keep DDoS (F1 $0.65$), so
the collapse follows from the label's temporal structure rather than from feature selection
(Section~\ref{sec:features}).

Two effects separate. The \emph{absolute} deterioration is large but comes from one class:
four-class weighted F1 falls from $0.933$ (random split) to $0.646$--$0.648$ for every model but SequentialCBM
(F1$_4$, main Table~\ref{M-tab:temporal}) because DDoS is lost; C\&C-HeartBeat also falls, from
$0.998$ to about $0.79$. This is a temporal-generalisation cost, not an interpretability one. The
\emph{between-model} gap is negligible: on the three coherent families every JointCBM variant,
HybridCBM and NeSy-NIDS match the MLP in aggregate (Coh-3 $0.862$--$0.863$) while redistributing
errors across classes (e.g.\ $\gamma{=}0$: Benign $0.841$ vs.\ $0.958$, Okiru $0.961$ vs.\ $0.844$).
SequentialCBM is the exception, losing Benign (F1$_4$ $0.330$, Coh-3 $0.441$). NeSy-NIDS gives the
highest temporal-split AUROC ($0.888{\pm}0.025$; $0.901{\pm}0.030$ with the $\alpha$-gate penalty of
Section~\ref{sec:alpha}), and the JointCBM $\gamma$ ordering shifts relative to the alternative split.

\section{Rule Selectivity and Threshold Drift}
\label{sec:selectivity}

All values in this section are mean binarised activations (Eq.~\ref{M-eq:binarise}) per
known class on the known-class validation split for the seed-0 model, the same data as
Fig.~\ref{M-fig:ruleactiv}. On CTU-IoT-23, several rules fire selectively but not deterministically
(\texttt{cc\_heartbeat\_beaconing} at $0.78$ and \texttt{incomplete\_handshake} at
$0.87$ on C\&C-HeartBeat, \texttt{asymmetric\_flood} at $0.81$ on DDoS and $0.13$ on Benign,
\texttt{ddos\_high\_rate} at $0.70$ on DDoS and $0.21$ on Okiru), leaving within-class
variance for Mahalanobis to work with; others fire across several classes
(\texttt{okiru\_low\_rate}: $0.79$ on Okiru but $0.87$ on Benign and $0.61$ on C\&C-HeartBeat). On CIC-IoT-2023 firing is
near-deterministic (\texttt{syn\_flood\_ddos} at $1.00$ on DDoS-SYN,
\texttt{benign\_low\_rate} at $0.97$ on Benign but also $0.91$ on Mirai), within-class variance is close to
zero, and Mahalanobis has little to separate on (AUROC $0.657$). These two activation
regimes coincide with the observed CTU-versus-CIC difference in Mahalanobis AUROC, but,
as the main paper establishes, known-class activation geometry does not by itself govern
detectability, so we report the pattern as descriptive, not causal
(Fig.~\ref{M-fig:ruleactiv}).
The learned thresholds move away from their expert initial values (seed~0): on
CTU-IoT-23 the \texttt{cc\_heartbeat\_beaconing} bounds move by up to $-0.50$, and on
CIC-IoT-2023 by up to $-0.88$ on \texttt{syn\_flood\_ddos}; across the five
seeds the per-condition drift vectors are correlated on CIC-IoT-2023 (mean pairwise $r{=}0.73$) but
not on CTU-IoT-23 ($r{=}0.05$; script \texttt{nesy/run\_fidelity.py}), so we read the CIC refinement as systematic
and the CTU pattern as indicative only (main Fig.~\ref{M-fig:threshdrift}).

\section{Configuration Sensitivity of the Geometry Correlations}
\label{sec:loco}

30 CBM models per dataset, which are five seeds of six configurations rather than 30
independent observations. Table~\ref{tab:loco} reports, on the same leak-free feature lists
as the main table (CTU: known-class-only selection, Section~\ref{sec:features}; CIC/TON:

correlation over the six configuration means ($r_6$, $n{=}6$), and the range of the 30-model
correlation when each configuration in turn is excluded (leave-one-configuration-out, LOCO).
On CIC-IoT-2023 and TON\_IoT the seven statistics agree in direction, and the six separability
and variance statistics are strongly associated with AUROC at the configuration-mean level
($|r_6|\ge0.93$ on both); closed-set F1 is weaker ($r_6{=}0.63$ and $0.73$) and is the only
statistic whose sign changes under LOCO there (on CIC). CTU-IoT-23 is different. All seven
statistics take the opposite sign ($r_6$ from $-0.82$ to $+0.49$), and the LOCO ranges show why:
every CTU range reaches its bound closest to zero when SequentialCBM is excluded, where the
correlations fall to $|r|\le0.22$ and Fisher ratio, silhouette and closed-set F1 change sign.
SequentialCBM combines the least separable concept space and the lowest closed-set F1 with one
of the highest CTU AUROCs, so this single configuration sets the CTU direction. The
unknown/known ratio, which uses the unknown flows, is positive everywhere ($r_6$ $0.70$--$0.92$).
These are associations with Mahalanobis AUROC measured \emph{within} each corpus, not a test that
a known-class statistic predicts AUROC on an unseen corpus or selects between scorers; the strong
$r_6$ values rest on six points, and the one selection procedure we tried, the leave-family-out
proxy of Section~\ref{sec:lofo}, did not reliably choose the scorer. The LOCO analysis thus
locates the CTU reversal in one configuration rather than in the corpus.

\section{CTU-IoT-23 Feature Derivation}
\label{sec:features}

CTU-IoT-23 is distributed as packet captures with labelled Zeek \texttt{conn.log} files. For
each capture we compute per-flow packet statistics with DPKT (field names follow the
CIC-IoT-2023 feature set) and join each labelled Zeek record to the DPKT flow with the same
source and destination IP and port whose start time lies in $[t_{\mathrm{zeek}},
t_{\mathrm{zeek}}{+}\mathrm{duration}{+}1\,\mathrm{s}]$; Zeek records without a match are
dropped. Four ratios are derived from Zeek fields: byte ratio
$\mathrm{orig\_bytes}/(\mathrm{resp\_bytes}{+}1)$, originator packet and byte rates
$\mathrm{orig\_pkts}/(\mathrm{duration}{+}1)$ and $\mathrm{orig\_ip\_bytes}/(\mathrm{duration}{+}1)$,
and direction $\mathrm{orig\_bytes}{-}\mathrm{resp\_bytes}$. The model uses 34 numeric features
(Zeek: duration, orig/resp bytes, packets and IP bytes; DPKT: Rate, Srate, IAT, Number,
Tot\_sum, Min, Max, AVG, Std, Variance, Magnitude, Radius, ack/syn/fin/rst counts,
fin/rst flag numbers, and HTTP, HTTPS, SSH, IRC, Telnet indicators; plus the four derived
ratios) and three Zeek categorical fields (conn\_state, history, service) integer-encoded, 37
in total. Missing values are set to $-1$; features are not rescaled.

\textbf{Feature-selection caveat.} The 34 numeric features were chosen before this study as
those covering 99\% of cumulative random-forest importance, with a class-weighted forest trained on
up to $10^6$ flows of every labelled CTU family. That sample included the nine
families later held out as unknown, so the CTU feature set is not blind to the held-out
labels. The choice is fixed and shared by every model compared, but it may favour
separability of the CTU unknowns in raw-feature space. CIC-IoT-2023 and TON\_IoT use fixed
feature lists (the 39 DPKT columns; 23 conn-log fields) without label-informed selection.

\textbf{Known-class feature-selection control.} Disclosing this is not enough, so we ran the
control. We re-selected the CTU numeric features with the same random-forest procedure but on
the known-class \emph{training} rows only, with the nine held-out families excluded from
selection. The known-only list keeps 28 numeric features (of 34): it drops the service and protocol
indicators (HTTP, HTTPS, SSH, IRC), two counters (\texttt{resp\_pkts}, \texttt{fin\_count})
and several statistical or derived features (Radius, Std, Variance, \texttt{resp\_ip\_bytes},
\texttt{rst\_flag\_number}), and adds TCP, Protocol\_Type, Time\_To\_Live and two TCP-flag indicators (\texttt{cwr}, \texttt{syn} flag numbers), 31 features in total with the three categorical fields. We drew a class-balanced split and
ran every CTU comparison with this list (\texttt{ctuk}); as a paired baseline we ran the
submitted 37-feature list on the \emph{same} rows (\texttt{ctuko}), so
\texttt{ctuko}\,vs\,\texttt{ctuk} isolates the selection source from the sample. The
NeSy-NIDS rule features are expert-defined rather than RF-selected, so the two rule counters the
known-only forest drops are restored and the rule indices remapped; only the neural branch and
the CBM/MLP/DT/RF inputs change. The script \texttt{06\_make\_kfs\_splits.py} and the drivers \texttt{cbm/run\_kfs\_control.py}
and \texttt{nesy/run\_kfs\_control.sh} reproduce the table.

Table~\ref{tab:kfs} reports the outcome over five seeds. The accuracy and scorer findings
hold. The JointCBM variants, HybridCBM and NeSy-NIDS still match the MLP on known-class F1
(all $0.933$); the two low-capacity models are unchanged (SequentialCBM $0.57$,
post-hoc CBM $0.58$). Per-class Mahalanobis is the best CTU scorer for every architecture under
both lists (Table~\ref{tab:kfs_scorer}), with the logit-energy score below chance for the MLP
($0.39$ vs.\ Mahalanobis $0.88$). Concept-fidelity regularisation $\gamma$ still has no
monotone effect on OOD AUROC (JointCBM $0.82$--$0.83$ and $0.81$--$0.84$ across $\gamma$;
Table~\ref{tab:kfs}). NeSy-NIDS, the strongest CTU detector, moves from $0.885$ to $0.904$.

Three OOD AUROC changes reach the $0.05$ level read as material elsewhere, and all are gains under
known-class-only selection. SequentialCBM rises from $0.782{\pm}.042$ to $0.872{\pm}.026$
($\Delta{=}+0.090$, beyond its seed std), HybridCBM from $0.772{\pm}.073$ to $0.822{\pm}.055$
($\Delta{=}+0.050$, below its own seed std) and Post-hoc CBM from $0.805$ to $0.871$; the MLP moves
$-0.020$, the JointCBM variants by at most $0.03$, and the DT and RF baselines gain $0.12$ and
$0.03$. Removing the held-out labels from feature selection therefore does not flatter the CTU
detection results; if anything the submitted list hurt the concept-space detectors.

The control also bears on the geometry reading. The CTU correlations of main
Table~\ref{M-tab:signflip} are opposite in sign to CIC-IoT-2023 and TON\_IoT, and
Section~\ref{sec:loco} traces the direction to SequentialCBM, the CBM configuration whose AUROC
changes most with the feature list. Under the leak-free list no CTU association holds without that
configuration, and we do not test whether any statistic predicts detectability on an unseen corpus
or selects a scorer.

\textbf{Per-split selection for the robustness experiments.} The known-only selection above is
fitted on the primary random split's known-class training rows, which is not automatically
leak-free for the other CTU experiments. We therefore run the same random-forest selection
separately on each: the alternative known set's training rows (excluding the now-held-out
C\&C-HeartBeat), the earliest 70\% of the timestamp-ordered split, and each leave-one-family-out
fold's reduced known set, so the family that experiment holds out, and any test-period flow, never
informs its features. Each of these splits draws its own class-balanced sample; running the main split's
31-feature list on the same sample separates re-selection from the sample, and the accuracy,
scorer and $\gamma$ conclusions hold under both. One consequence is visible in the temporal split
(Section~\ref{sec:temporal}): the DDoS collapse occurs under both feature lists (bar two JointCBM
seeds with the main-split list), so it is a property of the temporal split, not of feature
selection.

\section{Threshold Gradient}
\label{sec:gradient}

Training uses the soft activations of main Eq.~\ref{M-eq:rule_score} throughout, so every
threshold receives a gradient. For a greater-than condition $i\in\mathcal{I}_r^{>}$ of rule $r$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_i} =
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a_r} \cdot
    \Bigl(-k(t)\,g_i^{>}(1{-}g_i^{>})\prod_{j \neq i} g_j\Bigr),
\end{equation}
where $g_i^{>}{=}\sigma(k(t)(x_i{-}\theta_i))$ and $g_j$ are the other gates of the rule; the
sign flips for less-than conditions. As $k(t)$ grows, $g_i^{>}(1{-}g_i^{>})$ vanishes for gates
far from their threshold, so late in training only borderline conditions keep moving. The
straight-through estimator is not used in training.

\section{Leave-Family-Out Scorer Selection}
\label{sec:lofo}

The measured associations between known-class statistics and OOD AUROC vary across
datasets (main Table~\ref{M-tab:signflip}); whether a scorer can be \emph{selected} in advance is a separate
question, which we test directly with a \emph{behavioural} proxy: hold out one known attack
family, retrain JointCBM
($\gamma{=}0$) on the reduced known set, and keep the scorer (per-class Mahalanobis vs
logit-space energy) that best detects the held-out family, then ask whether that choice
matches the scorer actually best on the true unknowns (Mahalanobis on CTU-IoT-23, energy
on CIC-IoT-2023). We repeat for every known attack family $\times$ five seeds
(main Table~\ref{M-tab:lofo}).

The proxy is only \emph{conditionally} reliable. On CTU-IoT-23 the family-majority vote
recovers Mahalanobis (two of three families, $9/15$ folds); the C\&C-HeartBeat and DDoS
folds favour it, and only Okiru misfires (Mahalanobis cannot detect held-out Okiru,
$0.22$). On CIC-IoT-2023 the family-majority vote selects Mahalanobis for all four families
($15/20$ individual folds, $5/20$ energy) and so never recovers energy: held-out known families
remain separable in activation space (Mahalanobis $0.70$--$0.86$), so the proxy selects
Mahalanobis, yet the true CIC unknowns overlap the known boundary and need energy. Held-out-known thus
over-approximates activation-space detectability exactly on the corpus where the scorer
must switch. In these experiments, therefore, this leave-family-out proxy did not
consistently identify the best scorer; selecting it reliably
would require observing the novel regime, which we do not demonstrate a priori. This is a
further instance of the paper's negative result, here for scorer \emph{selection} rather
than scorer performance. Driver: \texttt{cbm/run\_lofo.py}.

\section{Open-Set (C+1) and Per-Family Evaluation}
\label{sec:openset_supp}

\textbf{Protocol.} We evaluate the deployed $(C{+}1)$-class detector at a reject threshold
$\tau$ calibrated to 5\% FPR on the known validation split. The scorer is per-class
Mahalanobis on the model's scoring space: the concept activations for CBM variants, the
binary rule activations for NeSy-NIDS, and the penultimate embedding for the MLP. Ground
truth has $C$ known classes plus a single ``unknown'' class into which \emph{all} held-out
families are merged; a flow is predicted ``unknown'' if its score exceeds $\tau$, else the
$\arg\max$ known-class logit. Evaluation uses $5{,}000$ known test flows (all seeds) and,
per unknown family, up to $3{,}000$ flows: $9$ families merged into ``unknown'' on
CTU-IoT-23 and $29$ on CIC-IoT-2023. Main Table~\ref{M-tab:openset_agg} reports, over five seeds,
the known-class rejection rate (target $\approx5\%$), the open-set macro-F1 over the $C{+}1$
classes, and the equal-family-weighted mean AUROC.

The known rejection rate is close to target ($4.2$--$5.8\%$). Across seeds the
coefficient of variation of $\tau$ is at most $0.11$ for every model ($0.09$ and $0.11$ for
NeSy-NIDS on CTU-IoT-23 and CIC-IoT-2023). Open-set macro-F1 is modest on CTU-IoT-23
($0.49$--$0.79$) and low on CIC-IoT-2023 ($0.13$--$0.27$). The mixture is essential to
reading this number. The evaluation pool is heavily unknown-skewed, for NeSy-NIDS on
CIC-IoT-2023 (Table~\ref{tab:confusion}, seed~0) it is $5{,}000$ known against $84{,}514$
unknown flows, about $16.9$ unknowns per known flow, and only $15{,}646$ ($18.5\%$) of the
pooled unknown flows exceed $\tau$. Macro-F1 weights every class equally, so it is \emph{not} dominated by the
unknown class; in that matrix the unknown class in fact has the \emph{highest} per-class
F1 ($\approx0.31$). What pulls macro-F1 down is a prevalence effect on the \emph{known}
classes: the ${\sim}81\%$ of unknown flows that are not rejected are absorbed into known
classes (most often VulnerabilityScan, Mirai, and DDoS-SYN), and because unknowns outnumber
knowns ${\sim}17{:}1$ these false positives collapse known-class \emph{precision} (Benign
precision $\approx0.07$, so F1 $\approx0.13$) even though known \emph{recall} stays high
for accepted flows. The low unknown recall is a genuine weakness, but the small macro-F1
is as much a prevalence artefact as a detection failure: reweighting the same confusion
matrix to equal known/unknown totals, with all conditional prediction rates unchanged,
raises macro-F1 from $\approx0.14$ to $\approx0.51$, an arithmetic sensitivity check, not
a better model. We therefore treat the operating-point macro-F1 as prevalence-sensitive
and base the detection claim on the (prevalence-free) per-family AUROC of
Table~\ref{tab:openset_perfam}.

\section{Joint \texorpdfstring{$(\lambda,\gamma)$}{(lambda,gamma)} Sweep}
\label{sec:lambdagamma}

predicted concepts toward the same labels, so we sweep them jointly (JointCBM, three seeds

is not a reliable lever: its effect on OOD AUROC is small ($|\Delta|\le0.046$) and its

has a larger effect, but only on CTU-IoT-23 (down the $\gamma{=}0$ column, $+0.092$), not on

sweep. (iii) Known-class F1 is nearly constant across the grid ($0.816$--$0.821$ on CIC; $0.932$--$0.933$

in the reported cell mean. It shows that heavy dual regularisation can destabilise
training; in the other 53 runs of the grid known-class F1 stays within the ranges above. The

instance of no single tunable factor governing open-set detectability.

\section{Concept-Fidelity Intervention (CBM)}
\label{sec:concept_intervention}

Comparing soft concepts with their binarised values cannot establish semantic fidelity,
since both can contain concept errors. Because our concepts are deterministic functions of
the input, we test directly how much the JointCBM's accuracy depends on \emph{deviations}
from the declared predicates. We (i) run the model normally (F1$_{\text{pred}}$), (ii)
intervene by replacing the predicted concepts with the \emph{true} binary predicates
$\mathbf{c}_{\text{gt}}$ and passing them through the model's own learned classifier head
(F1$_{\text{gt}}$), and (iii) fit a fresh logistic regression on $\mathbf{c}_{\text{gt}}$
alone (F1$_{\text{LR}}$, how well this linear classifier can classify from the declared
predicates; it is not an upper bound on the information they contain).

The gap is large (Table~\ref{tab:cintervene}). On CTU-IoT-23 the model scores F1 $0.93$ but
its classifier on the true predicates scores only $0.46$--$0.49$, and a logistic
regression on the declared predicates alone reaches $0.58$; on CIC-IoT-2023 the model
scores $0.82$ versus $0.62$--$0.65$ from true predicates and $0.72$ from a logistic
regression on the predicates alone. So the eight
declared predicates do not, under the evaluated linear classifiers, account for the CBM's
known-class accuracy. Because the intervention alters the classifier's input distribution,
this gap tests decision fidelity under predicate replacement; it does not attribute a fixed
fraction of accuracy to a source. What it shows is that the learned concept values, whether
through their continuous magnitudes or through their disagreements with the declared labels,
carry decision-relevant information that the declared predicates do not. On CTU-IoT-23 the
soft-versus-thresholded probe difference is small for $\gamma{\le}0.5$ (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}), so
there most of that information survives thresholding. The mean gap shrinks slightly with
$\gamma$ (CTU $0.476\to0.441$), but by about one seed std ($0.02$--$0.04$).

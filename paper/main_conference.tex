\begin{abstract}
Machine learning offers a promising way to detect cyberattacks in Internet of Things (IoT) networks. However, practical deployment is hindered when operators cannot understand model decisions, and unfamiliar attacks are misclassified as known types. We investigate whether interpretable models can address both challenges. We compare Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs), which predict through human-defined traffic concepts, with NeSy-NIDS, which learns thresholds for expert rules and combines their outputs with a neural network. On random splits of CTU-IoT-23 and CIC-IoT-2023, JointCBM and NeSy-NIDS achieve a weighted F1 within 0.002 of a standard neural network, averaged over five runs. On later CTU-IoT-23 traffic every model loses one attack class, while JointCBM, HybridCBM and NeSy-NIDS still track the baseline on the other classes.
The readable concepts and rules only partly explain predictions. Replacing a CBM's learned concept values with values derived directly from declared concept definitions reduces F1 from 0.933 to 0.457-0.492 on CTU-IoT-23. NeSy-NIDS agrees with its neural component on 97\% to 100\% of flows, but with its rule component on only 32\% to 55\%. Out-of-distribution detection similarly resists simple explanation: across CTU-IoT-23, CIC-IoT-2023, TON\_IoT, six CBM configurations, and five training runs, encouraging closer concept alignment has no consistent effect on AUROC. A CBM whose concept layer is trained before its classifier loses accuracy but detects unfamiliar CTU-IoT-23 attacks as well as the unconstrained network. Seven measurements derived from familiar traffic associate consistently with detection performance on CIC-IoT-2023 and TON\_IoT, but on CTU-IoT-23 their association is set by a single model configuration and disappears without it, so none is validated as a predictor. A method for selecting the scoring method from familiar traffic picked the wrong scorer for every held-out family on CIC-IoT-2023.
These findings highlight the need to evaluate interpretable intrusion detectors across multiple training runs, architectures, and datasets, with unfamiliar attack families excluded from feature selection and correlations checked for dependence on single configurations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Internet of Things (IoT) devices make up a large share of network endpoints, and their irregular updates and heterogeneous firmware make them attractive targets for botnet recruitment, DDoS amplification, and command-and-control (C\&C) infrastructure. Because IoT devices cannot run endpoint agents, detection has to work from flows captured at a gateway or router tap~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune}. The attack mix has recognizable signatures: Mirai-family botnets send high-rate UDP and TCP floods~\cite{meidan2018nbaiot}, C\&C heartbeats beacon at regular inter-arrival times, and reconnaissance scans produce asymmetric packet counts. Such signatures lend themselves to rule- and concept-based representations. At the same time, new Mirai sub-families and DDoS variants keep appearing, so a model trained on a handful of attack families will encounter structurally similar but unseen variants in deployment.

Deep-learning NIDS classify known attacks accurately but raise two problems in deployment. The first is \emph{opacity}: without knowing which traffic features drove a decision, operators cannot validate alerts, adjust policies, or meet regulatory requirements~\cite{rudin2019stop,arp2022dos}. Post-hoc explainers such as xNIDS~\cite{wei2023xnids} give more faithful explanations than LIME or SHAP, but they still explain a black box after the fact. The second is the \emph{open-set problem}: a NIDS trained on a closed set of attacks meets new families in deployment~\cite{arp2022dos} and confidently assigns them to known classes, and common remedies such as retraining or a separate anomaly detector do not use the model's own representation for open-set scoring~\cite{han2023owad}.

In this paper, we study both problems together by building interpretability into the architecture and measuring what that does to open-set detection. The evaluation framework is evaluated (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). In \textbf{CBMs}~\cite{koh2020concept}, predictions pass through a layer of human-defined flow concepts that can be inspected and overridden at test time. In \textbf{NeSy-NIDS}, expert attack signatures become threshold rules whose thresholds are learned through $k$-annealed sigmoid gates; a learned weight blends the rule head with a neural fallback, and a binarized rule vector is kept for audit and OOD scoring.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Accuracy without full fidelity.} Both interpretable architectures match an unconstrained MLP on known classes, but their declared concepts and rules only partly account for their predictions.
  \item \textbf{Geometry is associated with detectability, not a validated predictor.} Concept-fidelity regularisation has no monotone effect on OOD detection, and known-class geometry tracks OOD AUROC in a consistent direction on two of three corpora; on the third the association rests on a single configuration, so none of the statistics is yet a predictor.
  \item \textbf{Scorer selection remains open.} The best OOD scorer depends on both dataset and architecture, and a leave-family-out procedure selects it on one corpus but picks the wrong scorer for every held-out family on the other.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Scope.} The building blocks are established: concept bottlenecks~\cite{koh2020concept}, sigmoid-relaxed threshold rules~\cite{petersen2022logic,yang2022ste}, per-class Mahalanobis scoring~\cite{lee2018simple}, and neural/rule hybridisation. We claim no new state-of-the-art score. The contribution is empirical and methodological: a controlled characterisation of where interpretable open-set IoT NIDS hold up and where they do not, with evaluation guidance in the spirit of~\cite{arp2022dos}. We make no streaming, concept-drift, or false-alert-rate claims.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

Post-hoc explanation is the dominant answer to NIDS opacity: xNIDS~\cite{wei2023xnids} uses sparse group lasso to produce human-readable firewall rules, and GEAD~\cite{han2024gead} extracts global rules from a trained deep model. The explanation lives outside the model and can stop matching it when the model changes, so we build interpretability into the architecture instead.

CBMs~\cite{koh2020concept} force predictions through human-specified concepts. Concept embedding models~\cite{zarlenga2022concept} extend concepts to higher-dimensional embeddings, and a trained network can be converted into a CBM post hoc~\cite{yuksekgonul2023posthoc}. Concept bottlenecks are known to leak: Mahinpei et al.~\cite{mahinpei2021promises} show that concept models carry predictive information their labels do not capture. Our fidelity tests reproduce this on IoT flows; what we add is its relation to the rule-branch dependence of a parallel neuro-symbolic model and to open-set detectability, under one multi-seed, multi-architecture, multi-dataset protocol. Closest to our setting, Shoukat et al.~\cite{shoukat2026concept} apply a concept bottleneck with prototype reasoning to intrusion detection in SDN-based industrial networks; we differ in evaluating open-set detection on IoT flows, comparing against a neuro-symbolic rule model, and measuring decision fidelity and operating-threshold behaviour.

Petersen et al.~\cite{petersen2022logic} train binary logic-gate networks through smooth relaxations, and Yang et al.~\cite{yang2022ste} inject logical constraints with the straight-through estimator~\cite{bengio2013ste}. NeSy-NIDS applies this line of work to IoT flows, where rules are threshold conjunctions over flow features that an analyst can read and override.

The Mahalanobis detector~\cite{lee2018simple} fits class-conditional Gaussians to learned features, and Fort et al.~\cite{fort2021exploring} found that feature quality matters more for OOD performance than the scoring rule. Softmax-based alternatives include maximum softmax probability~\cite{hendrycks2017baseline} and the energy score~\cite{liu2020energy}. Vaze et al.~\cite{vaze2022openset} report that on visual-recognition benchmarks a better closed-set classifier tends to give better open-set recognition; we test whether this empirical relationship transfers to IoT flows. In security, OWAD~\cite{han2023owad} detects normality shift and CADE~\cite{yang2021cade} isolates drifting samples, both at the system level, whereas we score individual flows. N-BaIoT~\cite{meidan2018nbaiot} and Kitsune~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune} established per-device anomaly detection for IoT but neither classifies attacks nor explains its decisions. Arp et al.~\cite{arp2022dos} and Apruzzese et al.~\cite{apruzzese2022ml} identify closed-world evaluation and opacity as obstacles to deployment, and De Keersmaeker et al.~\cite{dekeersmaeker2023survey} survey the public IoT security datasets.

\subsection{Datasets and Protocol}
\label{sec:datasets}

\textbf{CTU-IoT-23}~\cite{parmisano2020ctu} provides labelled flows from IoT devices captured in a laboratory. Each Zeek conn-log record is joined to per-flow statistics extracted from the packet captures; we use 31 of the resulting features (28 numeric, 3 categorical), selected on known-class training flows only. \textbf{CIC-IoT-2023}~\cite{neto2023ciciot} covers 33 attack scenarios across 105 devices; we use 39 of its flow features. \textbf{TON\_IoT}~\cite{alsaedi2020toniot} is an independent testbed whose network subset is derived from Zeek logs; we use 23 features from its connection and protocol logs. TON\_IoT enters only the CBM concept-space analysis (Section~\ref{sec:ksweep}), as a third corpus. Its flow logs carry no per-flag counts or inter-arrival times, so three CTU concepts that need them are replaced by connection-state, responsiveness and web-service concepts.

Table~\ref{tab:protocol} summarises the protocol. Known classes are class-balanced by random sampling, and every held-out family is excluded from training and validation. The split and the OOD evaluation subset are the same for all seeds.

The known sets are: CTU \{Benign, C\&C-HeartBeat, DDoS, Okiru\}; CIC \{Benign, DDoS-SYN\_Flood, Mirai-greeth\_flood, Recon-PortScan, VulnerabilityScan\}; TON \{normal, scanning, DDoS, password, injection\}. The held-out families are, for CTU, C\&C, Attack, C\&C-HeartBeat-Attack, C\&C-FileDownload, C\&C-Torii, C\&C-Mirai, C\&C-HeartBeat-FileDownload, FileDownload and Okiru-Attack; for CIC, the dataset's other 29 attack classes (DDoS, DoS, Mirai, reconnaissance, spoofing, web-attack, brute-force and backdoor classes); and for TON, DoS, XSS, backdoor, ransomware and MITM. Each known set is a benign baseline plus one representative family from each dominant malicious category in the dataset (e.g.\ volumetric DDoS, botnet C\&C or Mirai-family traffic, and scanning where the dataset has it; TON adds credential and web attacks), the traffic an operator could plausibly label early in a deployment. Most held-out families are sub-families and protocol variants of these categories, which is the operational open-set case.

\textbf{Preprocessing.} CIC and TON numeric features are standardised with a scaler fit on the training split. CTU features enter in raw units (missing values set to $-1$), with categorical fields integer-encoded from vocabularies built on known-class flows. Concept and rule thresholds were written from documented attack signatures and known-class statistics. No preprocessing statistic or concept or rule threshold uses a held-out family. The submitted CTU list (37 features) was an exception: it was selected by random-forest importance on a sample that included every CTU label, the held-out families among them, which may favour CTU OOD separability (CIC and TON use fixed lists). All CTU results here therefore use the same selection re-run on known-class training rows only (31 features; NeSy-NIDS adds back the two counters its expert rules read), and the submitted list is kept as an ablation. The re-selection leaves the accuracy and open-set findings in place, and it raises rather than lowers the AUROC of the two configurations that are most sensitive to the feature list (Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:features}, Table~\ref{tab:kfs}). Every split and evaluation subset is drawn deterministically by the scripts we release.

\textbf{Robustness splits.} We repeat the CBM evaluation on an alternative known set per dataset (CTU \{Benign, DDoS, Okiru, C\&C\}, promoting C\&C to known and demoting C\&C-HeartBeat to unknown; CIC \{Benign, DDoS-ICMP\_Flood, Mirai-udpplain, Recon-PortScan, VulnerabilityScan\}) and on a timestamp-ordered CTU split, in which each known class is ordered by capture time and the earliest 70\% trains and the latest 15\% tests. For CTU, the feature list is re-selected by the same random-forest procedure on each experiment's own known-class training rows, so the family it holds out (and any test-period flow) never informs its features. Each of these splits draws its own class-balanced sample; a control that uses the main split's 31-feature list on the same sample separates the effect of re-selection from that of the sample, and the accuracy and open-set conclusions hold under both.

\subsection{Concept Bottleneck Models}
\label{sec:cbm}

A CBM~\cite{koh2020concept} factors the classifier into two stages,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{c}} = g(\mathbf{x}),
  \quad\hat{y} = h(\hat{\mathbf{c}}),
\end{equation}
where $g:\mathbb{R}^d\to[0,1]^K$ is the concept encoder (an MLP $d{\to}256{\to}256{\to}64{\to}K$ with LayerNorm and ReLU) and $h:[0,1]^K\to\mathbb{R}^C$ is a linear classifier over the $C$ known classes. We define $K{=}8$ concepts per dataset (e.g.\ \textit{is\_high\_rate}: Rate${>}1000$\,pps; \textit{is\_beaconing}: $1.5{<}$IAT${<}5$\,s $\wedge$ $0.1{<}$Rate${<}5$; \textit{is\_incomplete\_handshake}: syn\_count${>}2$ $\wedge$ fin\_count${=}0$), each a binary label produced by a threshold rule on the raw features (Table~\ref{tab:concepts}). Because the labels are deterministic functions of the input, \emph{concept accuracy} measures how faithfully the bottleneck reproduces these declared predicates, not whether the predicates are semantically correct. $K{=}8$ balances vocabulary against the sample size the shared-covariance Mahalanobis fit needs.

\textbf{Training variants.} \emph{JointCBM} minimises
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}} + \lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}
    + \frac{\gamma}{K}\,\|\hat{\mathbf{c}} - \mathbf{c}_{\text{gt}}\|^2,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{c}_{\text{gt}}\in\{0,1\}^K$ are the concept labels, $\lambda$ weights concept supervision, and $\gamma\in\{0,0.1,0.5,1.0\}$ weights an additional squared-error concept-fidelity term. Both terms push the predicted concepts toward the same labels. \emph{SequentialCBM} trains and freezes $g$ on the concept labels before training $h$. \emph{HybridCBM} concatenates the 64-dimensional encoder embedding with the $K$ concepts as classifier input, so the classifier can use information outside the concept vocabulary. \emph{Post-hoc CBM} fits a logistic-regression probe per concept to the frozen MLP embedding and a logistic-regression classifier to the probe outputs, both on the training split.

\textbf{OOD scoring.} We apply per-class Mahalanobis scoring~\cite{lee2018simple} in concept space. Class means $\boldsymbol{\mu}_c$ and a shared covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ ($\varepsilon{=}10^{-4}$ ridge, pseudoinverse) are fitted to training concept vectors, and the anomaly score is
\begin{equation}
  s(\mathbf{x}) = \min_c\,(\hat{\mathbf{c}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_c)^\top
  \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\hat{\mathbf{c}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_c).
  \label{eq:mahal}
\end{equation}

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{CBM Training and OOD Scoring}
\label{alg:cbm}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\footnotesize
\Require $\{(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{c}_i,y_i)\}$, $\lambda$, $\gamma$
\State Initialise encoder $g$ ($d{\to}256{\to}256{\to}64{\to}K$, LayerNorm+ReLU), linear classifier $h$
\For{each epoch and mini-batch $(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Y})$}
  \State $\hat{\mathbf{C}}{=}g(\mathbf{X})$;\; $\mathcal{L}{=}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(h(\hat{\mathbf{C}}),\mathbf{Y}){+}\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}(\hat{\mathbf{C}},\mathbf{C}){+}\tfrac{\gamma}{K}\|\hat{\mathbf{C}}{-}\mathbf{C}\|^2$;\; update $g,h$
\EndFor
\State $\boldsymbol{\mu}_c{=}\mathrm{mean}\{g(\mathbf{x}_i){:}\,y_i{=}c\}$;\; $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{=}$ regularised shared covariance
\State \textbf{Inference:} $\hat{\mathbf{c}}{=}g(\mathbf{x})$;\; $\hat{y}{=}\arg\max h(\hat{\mathbf{c}})$;\; $s(\mathbf{x})$ by Eq.~\ref{eq:mahal}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{NeSy-NIDS: Differentiable Rule Learning}
\label{sec:nesy}

\textbf{Rule templates.} The $R{=}8$ rule templates per dataset are written from domain knowledge. Each is a conjunction of threshold conditions; representative CTU-IoT-23 rules are \texttt{ddos\_short\_burst} (Rate${>}1000$ $\wedge$ duration${<}0.1$; DDoS), \texttt{okiru\_low\_rate} (Rate${<}500$ $\wedge$ duration${<}2.5$; Okiru) and \texttt{benign\_single\_probe} (orig\_pkts${<}2$ $\wedge$ Rate${<}100$; Benign), and several rules start from concept thresholds (e.g.\ \texttt{cc\_heartbeat\_beaconing} from \textit{is\_beaconing}). The full banks with initial thresholds and declared target classes are in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:rulebank}. Let $\mathcal{I}_r^{>}$ and $\mathcal{I}_r^{<}$ index the greater-than and less-than conditions of rule $r$. Following~\cite{petersen2022logic}, each indicator is replaced by a sigmoid gate and the conjunction by a product:
\begin{equation}
  a_r(\mathbf{x};k) =
    \prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}_r^{>}}\!\sigma\!\bigl(k(x_i{-}\theta_i)\bigr)\cdot
    \prod_{j\in\mathcal{I}_r^{<}}\!\sigma\!\bigl(k(\theta_j{-}x_j)\bigr),
  \label{eq:rule_score}
\end{equation}
so the thresholds $\theta$ receive gradients throughout training.

\textbf{Annealing and binarisation.} The steepness $k$ rises linearly from $k_0{=}1$ to $k_T{=}10$ over $T_{\mathrm{warm}}{=}30$ epochs, $k(t) = k_0 + \min(t/T_{\mathrm{warm}},1)(k_T{-}k_0)$. Training and classification use the soft activations of Eq.~\ref{eq:rule_score}. For audit and OOD scoring, each condition is thresholded and the rule is their logical conjunction,
\begin{equation}
  a_r^{\mathrm{hard}} = \prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}_r^{>}}\mathbf{1}[x_i{>}\theta_i]\;
                        \prod_{j\in\mathcal{I}_r^{<}}\mathbf{1}[x_j{<}\theta_j],
  \label{eq:binarise}
\end{equation}
which is not the same operation as thresholding the soft product.

\textbf{Architecture and rule-head weight.} A linear rule head maps the soft activations, scaled by learned per-rule importance weights, to rule logits $\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{rule}}$. A parallel MLP (hidden layers of 128 and 64 units, LayerNorm and ReLU) on the raw features produces neural logits $\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{neural}}$, and
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{l} = \alpha\,\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{rule}} +
               (1{-}\alpha)\,\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{neural}},\qquad \alpha{=}\sigma(w_\alpha),
  \label{eq:alpha_gate}
\end{equation}
with $w_\alpha$ learned jointly. An optional penalty $\lambda_\alpha(1{-}\alpha)^2$ pushes $\alpha$ upward. $\alpha$ is a single global mixing weight; because the two heads can emit logits of different magnitudes, its value alone does not measure how much the prediction relies on the rules (Section~\ref{sec:fidelity}).

\textbf{OOD scoring in rule space.} The binary vector $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{hard}}(\mathbf{x})\in\{0,1\}^R$ is scored with Eq.~\ref{eq:mahal}, fitted on known-class training activations. Binary vectors are not Gaussian, so this is an approximation; Section~\ref{sec:ood} compares it with binary-native and logit-space scorers.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{NeSy-NIDS Training and OOD Scoring}
\label{alg:nesy}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\footnotesize
\Require $\{(\mathbf{x}_i,y_i)\}$, templates with initial thresholds $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}$, $\lambda_\alpha$, $k_0{=}1$, $k_T{=}10$, $T_{\mathrm{warm}}$
\State Initialise $\boldsymbol{\theta}{=}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}$, rule head $(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{imp}})$, MLP $\phi$, gate $w_\alpha$
\For{epoch $t=1,\ldots,T$}
  \State $k{=}k_0{+}\min(t/T_{\mathrm{warm}},1)(k_T{-}k_0)$
  \For{each mini-batch $(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})$}
    \State $\mathbf{a}{=}\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{X};k)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:rule_score}, soft);\; $\mathbf{l}{=}\alpha\,\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{rule}}(\mathbf{a}){+}(1{-}\alpha)\,\mathrm{MLP}_\phi(\mathbf{X})$, $\alpha{=}\sigma(w_\alpha)$
    \State $\mathcal{L}{=}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{Y}){+}\lambda_\alpha(1{-}\alpha)^2$;\; update $\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{imp}},\phi,w_\alpha$
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State Fit $\boldsymbol{\mu}_c$, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ on $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{hard}}(\mathbf{x}_i)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:binarise}) of the training flows
\State \textbf{Inference:} classify $\hat{y}{=}\arg\max\mathbf{l}$ from soft $\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{x};k_T)$; score $s(\mathbf{x})$ by Eq.~\ref{eq:mahal} on $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{hard}}(\mathbf{x})$ (OOD and audit)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Open-Set Decision Rule}
\label{sec:openset}

Both classifiers are closed-set, so we couple the anomaly score to the classifier through a rejection threshold $\tau$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y}_{\mathrm{open}}(\mathbf{x}) =
  \begin{cases}
    \textit{unknown}, & s(\mathbf{x}) > \tau,\\[2pt]
    \arg\max_c\, l_c(\mathbf{x}), & \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $l_c$ is the $c$-th class logit. $\tau$ is the 95th percentile of scores on the known-class validation split, so about 5\% of known traffic is rejected; no unknown flow is used to set it.

\subsection{Baselines, Metrics and Training}
\label{sec:eval}

The MLP baseline has the same encoder as the CBM followed by a linear classifier and is scored with Mahalanobis on its 64-dimensional embedding. A decision tree (DT; depth chosen from $\{5,10,15,\infty\}$ by validation F1) is paired with per-class Mahalanobis on the raw training features, and a random forest (RF~\cite{breiman2001random}; 100 trees, depth 10) is scored with $1{-}\max(\hat{p})$. We report weighted F1 on the known test split, and AUROC and TPR at 5\% FPR with known test flows as negatives and held-out unknown flows as positives. All hyperparameters and early stopping use the known-class validation split. Adam ($\mathrm{lr}{=}10^{-3}$, batch 512); CBMs train for up to 50 epochs (patience 10) with $\lambda{=}0.5$, chosen from $\{0.1,0.5,1.0\}$ by known-class validation F1, which varies by at most $0.002$ across that grid; on the original-feature splits, raising $\lambda$ from $0.1$ to $1.0$ raises concept accuracy on both datasets but moves OOD AUROC in opposite directions (CTU $0.790{\to}0.884$, CIC $0.752{\to}0.724$, five seeds; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:lambda}); NeSy-NIDS trains for up to 60 epochs (patience 12) with $\lambda_\alpha\in\{0,0.1\}$.

\textbf{Evaluation subsets.} Each analysis draws one evaluation subset and reuses it for every model and seed it compares; the NeSy scorer ablation (Table~\ref{tab:scorer}) scores all five scorers on a subset drawn per seed. Different analyses draw independently under the same 5{,}000/5{,}000 protocol, so the same model can differ slightly between tables. For the CIC MLP, Table~\ref{tab:main} gives $0.807{\pm}.007$ (five seeds), the scorer\,$\times$\,architecture analysis $0.807{\pm}.008$ (five seeds, another subset), and the post-hoc detector comparison $0.815$ (the seed-0 model on a third subset). These subsets span $0.807$--$0.815$, a range of $0.008$ that is below the cross-architecture and cross-scorer gaps we interpret ($\ge0.05$), and we compare values only within one analysis.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Classification Accuracy}
\label{sec:acc}

\begin{sloppypar}\hbadness=10000
Table~\ref{tab:main} shows NeSy-NIDS and every JointCBM variant tracking the MLP on both datasets. Paired per-seed F1 differences from the MLP have means of at most $0.0001$ in magnitude on CTU-IoT-23 (no seed beyond $0.0004$) and $-0.0004$ to $+0.0002$ on CIC-IoT-2023 (no seed beyond $0.0026$). These are small observed differences, well inside seed variation, not a demonstration of statistical equivalence. For JointCBM ($\gamma{=}0$) the match also holds per class: every class is within $0.002$ of the MLP. The weighted F1 hides a spread across classes: the weakest are CTU Okiru ($0.86$) and DDoS ($0.90$), and CIC Recon-PortScan ($0.64$), VulnerabilityScan ($0.70$) and Benign ($0.76$).
\end{sloppypar}

The rule-only head reaches only $0.566$ on CTU-IoT-23 and $0.750$ on CIC-IoT-2023, so the neural fallback covers traffic the eight rule templates do not. SequentialCBM and Post-hoc CBM lose much of their accuracy (CTU $0.567$/$0.576$; CIC $0.732$/$0.746$): this is consistent with a linear classifier over concepts learned separately from the class labels being unable to recover the class boundary. The $\gamma$ variants have near-identical F1 in the main runs; one heavily regularised run in the supplementary $(\lambda,\gamma)$ grid ($\lambda{=}1.0$, $\gamma{=}0.5$, seed~0) fell to F1 $0.748$.

\textbf{Robustness.} On the alternative known sets the MLP reaches F1 $0.935$ (CTU) and $0.820$ (CIC), and every JointCBM variant matches it within $0.001$ on both. The timestamp-ordered split lowers four-class weighted F1 from $0.933$ to $0.646$--$0.648$ for every model except SequentialCBM ($0.330$; Table~\ref{tab:temporal}). The CTU \emph{DDoS} label spans two capture regimes (a high-rate flood, then a near-zero-rate capture), so the temporal split trains on one and tests on the other, and every model scores F1 $0.00$ on DDoS on every seed; on the same sample with the main split's 31-feature list DDoS also collapses for every model and seed except two JointCBM $\gamma{=}0.5$ seeds, so the cause is the label's temporal structure, not feature selection. On the three temporally-coherent families the interpretable models match the MLP in aggregate (Coh-3 $0.862$--$0.863$) while trading per-class strengths (JointCBM $\gamma{=}0$: Benign $0.841$ vs.\ $0.958$, Okiru $0.961$ vs.\ $0.844$); only SequentialCBM, which loses Benign, falls behind. The accuracy match is therefore a matched-distribution result.

\subsection{Open-Set Detection}
\label{sec:ood}

\textbf{CTU-IoT-23.} Under the leak-free feature selection the highest observed means come from NeSy-NIDS with the $\alpha$ penalty ($0.909{\pm}.014$) and NeSy-NIDS ($0.904{\pm}.014$), followed by the MLP ($0.874{\pm}.002$), SequentialCBM ($0.872{\pm}.026$) and Post-hoc CBM ($0.871{\pm}.024$); the NeSy-MLP gap is about two seed std. The four JointCBM variants reach $0.81$--$0.84$ (seed std $0.04$--$0.05$), HybridCBM $0.822$, and the DT and RF baselines $0.679$ and $0.657$. SequentialCBM, whose concept head is trained on the concept labels alone and frozen, detects as well as the MLP while its closed-set F1 is only $0.567$: on CTU a sequentially trained bottleneck gives up accuracy but not open-set detection. With the originally submitted feature list on the same rows, SequentialCBM and HybridCBM reach only $0.782$ and $0.772$, so known-class-only selection raises them (ablation, Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:kfs}).

\textbf{CIC-IoT-2023.} The MLP ($0.807{\pm}.007$) and the DT's raw-feature Mahalanobis ($0.802$) have the highest AUROC. The JointCBM variants reach $0.699$--$0.717$; SequentialCBM and HybridCBM reach $0.667$ and $0.669$, and NeSy-NIDS with Mahalanobis only $0.657$ ($0.691$ with the $\alpha$ penalty). Unlike on CTU, SequentialCBM is among the weakest detectors here. The low-dimensional concept and rule spaces separate these unknowns less well than the 64-dimensional MLP embedding and 39 raw features, though we ran no dimension-matched control and do not attribute the gap to dimensionality alone.

\textbf{Concept-fidelity regularisation is not a lever.} The sign of $\Delta{=}\gamma_{1.0}{-}\gamma_0$ is opposite on the two main splits, and across five dataset/split combinations it is positive on two and negative on three, with $|\Delta|\le0.06$ (Table~\ref{tab:gamma_splits}). In a joint $(\lambda,\gamma)$ grid (three seeds per cell; Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:lambdagamma}) the sign of the $\gamma$ effect also depends on $\lambda$. An effect of this size can be read off a single configuration and a single seed, so claims about it need multi-seed replication under a fixed scoring protocol.

\textbf{Which scorer works depends on the representation.} Table~\ref{tab:scorer} compares per-class Mahalanobis on the NeSy rule vector with two binary-native distances to the nearest known-class centroid (Hamming, Jaccard), the logit-space energy score $-\log\sum_c e^{l_c}$~\cite{liu2020energy}, and a $z$-normalised Mahalanobis$+$energy average. On CTU the two rule-vector distances and per-class Mahalanobis all work ($0.86$--$0.90$) while energy is below chance ($0.46$). On CIC no activation-space scorer exceeds $0.66$ and only the energy-based scorers detect ($0.747$--$0.763$). The Gaussian assumption is therefore not what limits the binary rule space; whether that space separates known from unknown flows at all is. Running Mahalanobis and energy on the MLP and all six CBM configurations shows that the ranking depends on architecture as well (Table~\ref{tab:scorer_arch}). On CTU Mahalanobis is better for every architecture, by $0.23$--$0.49$. On CIC energy is better for the four JointCBM variants ($0.732$--$0.769$ vs.\ $0.702$--$0.722$), while Mahalanobis stays better for the MLP, SequentialCBM and HybridCBM. No scorer is best across both datasets and all architectures.

\subsection{Fidelity of the Concept and Rule Representations}
\label{sec:fidelity}

Readable concepts or rules need not account for the predictions made from them, so we test fidelity separately from accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:fidelity}). \textbf{CBM.} Three representations must be kept apart: the soft predicted concepts, their thresholded values, and the declared predicates used as labels. A linear probe on the soft predicted concepts gains little over one on their thresholded values on CTU-IoT-23 (except for two $\gamma{=}1.0$ seeds) and more on CIC-IoT-2023, where the gain grows rather than shrinks with $\gamma$ (Table~\ref{tab:fidelity}). A small gain does not establish fidelity, because thresholded predictions can disagree with the declared predicates and still carry class information. We therefore also pass the declared predicates through the model's own classifier, and train a logistic regression on them directly. Both fall far below the learned concepts (on CTU the replacement F1 has seed std $0.02$--$0.04$), so under the evaluated linear classifiers the declared predicates do not reproduce the model's accuracy. Replacement also changes the classifier's input distribution, so these drops test decision fidelity under predicate replacement; they are not a decomposition of accuracy by source. Raising $\gamma$ improves concept-label agreement on CTU ($87.4\%$ to $94.2\%$; Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:leakage}) but leaves the gap within seed variability.

\subsection{Known-Class Geometry: Consistent on Two Corpora, Configuration-Driven on CTU}
\label{sec:ksweep}

Across the ten NeSy-NIDS models, mean within-class rule-activation variance tracks OOD AUROC ($r{=}0.971$), but the models form two dataset clusters, so this correlation cannot separate a property of the representation from a property of the dataset. We therefore test known-class statistics across the thirty CBM concept-space models per dataset (six configurations $\times$ five seeds) on three corpora.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/confounding.pdf}
  \caption{Within-class concept-activation variance vs.\ OOD AUROC for the CBM concept-space models, on leak-free feature lists (CTU: known-class-only selection, Section~\ref{sec:features}; CIC/TON: fixed). Colour: dataset; marker: configuration; faint points: seeds; solid points: configuration means ($\pm$1 seed std); dashed: per-dataset fits; stars: dataset means. Slopes are negative on CIC and TON; on CTU the weakly positive slope is set by SequentialCBM (top right), and the dataset means are non-monotone.}
  \label{fig:confounding}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Within-class variance.} On CIC-IoT-2023 and TON\_IoT more within-class variance goes with worse detection ($r{=}-0.77$ and $-0.46$). On CTU-IoT-23 the slope is weakly \emph{positive} ($+0.24$) and comes from SequentialCBM, which shares the highest within-class variance with HybridCBM but is also the strongest CTU concept-space detector ($0.872$, against $0.822$ for HybridCBM; Fig.~\ref{fig:confounding}). Without SequentialCBM the CTU correlation is $+0.01$. The within-dataset direction also does not carry across corpora: the three dataset means are non-monotone ($r{=}+0.36$ over three points), so a corpus's mean known-class variance does not rank its detectability.

\textbf{Seven statistics.} We tested seven statistics computed from known classes only: within-class variance, Fisher trace ratio $\mathrm{tr}(S_B)/\mathrm{tr}(S_W)$, ridge-LDA separability $\mathrm{tr}((S_W{+}\varepsilon I)^{-1}S_B)$, silhouette, nearest-centroid margin, concept-activation entropy, and closed-set F1. On CIC-IoT-2023 and TON\_IoT each keeps the same sign (Table~\ref{tab:signflip}): more known-class separability, or less within-class variance, goes with higher OOD AUROC, as intuition and Vaze et al.~\cite{vaze2022openset} predict, and at the configuration-mean level the six separability and variance statistics reach $|r_6|\ge0.93$ on both. On CTU-IoT-23 all seven take the opposite sign at the model level ($|r|\le0.37$), and leaving out SequentialCBM shrinks them to $|r|\le0.22$, with three of them changing sign (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:loco}). SequentialCBM pairs the lowest closed-set F1 and the least separable concept space with one of the highest CTU AUROCs, so this one configuration sets the CTU direction. The leave-one-configuration-out check shows that the CTU reversal is a property of one configuration rather than of the corpus. Correlations pooled over configurations, as here, therefore need such a check before they are read as a property of a dataset. These are within-corpus associations with Mahalanobis AUROC, not a test that a statistic predicts AUROC on an unseen corpus or selects a scorer; the one predictive procedure we tried (leave-family-out) did not (Section~\ref{sec:ood}).

\textbf{A manipulation and an interpretation.} Retraining NeSy-NIDS at annealing steepness $k_T\in\{2,5,10,20\}$ (five seeds, constant known-class F1) raises within-class variance on CIC ($0.024{\to}0.037$) but AUROC follows only weakly ($r{=}0.32$, $0.63$ at $k_T{\le}5$ vs.\ $0.66$ at $k_T{\ge}10$); on CTU variance barely moves ($0.125$--$0.128$) and is not positively associated with AUROC ($r{=}-0.15$). A structural argument is consistent with this: a known-class-conditional scorer separates unknowns by where they fall relative to the known class model, which depends on the unknown flows as well as the representation, so a known-class statistic alone need not determine it. We state the result narrowly, for the settings tested.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Deployment implications.} Rejection-threshold calibration and scorer selection are separate problems: the threshold $\tau$ needs only known validation traffic and held the known rejection rate near its target (Section~\ref{sec:ood}), whereas choosing the scorer does not reduce to known traffic in the same way (limitation~(iii)). For model tuning, $\gamma$ offers no reliable OOD benefit, so effort is better spent matching the scorer to the representation and reading the concept or rule vector alongside the fidelity limits of Section~\ref{sec:fidelity}.

\phantomsection\label{sec:compute}
\textbf{Computational cost.} On CTU-IoT-23, JointCBM adds 296 parameters to the MLP and NeSy-NIDS has 13{,}313, all under $0.4$\,MB in \texttt{float32}. Per-flow (batch-1) latency on one thread of an Intel Core i9-9820X is $96$\,$\mu$s (MLP), $109$\,$\mu$s (JointCBM) and about $590$\,$\mu$s (NeSy-NIDS, an un-vectorised per-rule loop), measured with the submitted 37-feature input.

\textbf{Limitations.} (i)~\emph{Authored vocabulary.} The concepts and rules were written by us from documented attack signatures~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune,meidan2018nbaiot} and not validated by operators or independent experts. (ii)~\emph{Capture overlap and feature selection.} Splits are random at the flow level; capture-, session- and device-disjoint evaluation remains untested, and the few source hosts per CTU family (4--7) make it harder to construct. The CTU feature set was selected with the held-out labels visible; re-selecting without them per split leaves the findings in place. Each split is a single class-balanced sample, so multi-sample or full-corpus evaluation is a natural next step (Sections~\ref{sec:datasets},~\ref{sec:features}). (iii)~\emph{Scorer selection.} No procedure we tested selects the best scorer from known traffic alone (Section~\ref{sec:ood}). (iv)~\emph{Timing} was measured only on a desktop CPU. (v)~\emph{Coverage.} The post-hoc detector comparison uses the seed-0 MLP and the $(\lambda,\gamma)$ grid three seeds; NeSy-NIDS and the temporal/alternative splits cover two datasets, the geometry three. All corpora are fixed-vector flow representations with hand-authored concepts, so we make no claim for packet-sequence, graph, or learned-concept representations.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Interpretable IoT intrusion detectors can match an unconstrained MLP on known classes, but their inspectable concepts and rules do not account for their decisions, and their open-set detection depends on dataset, architecture and scorer; the known-class statistics we measured are associated with detectability on two corpora, through a single configuration on the third, and none is validated as a predictor. Evaluations of interpretable NIDS should report decision fidelity alongside accuracy, support open-set claims with multiple seeds, architectures, datasets and leak-free feature selection, and check pooled correlations with a leave-one-configuration-out analysis; the most important remaining test is a capture-disjoint evaluation.

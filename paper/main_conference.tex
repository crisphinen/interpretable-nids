\begin{abstract}
Machine learning can detect attacks in Internet of Things (IoT) networks, but operators cannot see why a model raises an alert, and new attacks pass as known ones. We test whether interpretable models solve both. We compare Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs), classifying through human-defined concepts, with NeSy-NIDS, which learns thresholds for expert-written rules alongside a neural network. On CTU-IoT-23 and CIC-IoT-2023, the jointly trained CBMs and NeSy-NIDS classify known traffic as accurately as a neural network. On later CTU-IoT-23 traffic every tested model misses one attack class, and on held-out CTU-IoT-23 source addresses the learned models' mean F1 falls to 0.56 or below, unlike on TON\_IoT. Explanations only partly account for decisions: giving a CBM its declared concept values instead of learned ones lowers F1 from 0.933 to below 0.50, and NeSy-NIDS's class predictions agree with its neural part on 97--100\% of flows but with its rules on only 32--55\%. For unfamiliar attacks, pulling concepts closer to their definitions has no consistent effect, the best detection score depends on dataset and model, and two ways of choosing it from familiar traffic each failed on a dataset. A CBM that learns concepts before its classifier loses accuracy but detects unfamiliar CTU-IoT-23 attacks about as well as the network. Seven measures of how well familiar classes separate track detection on CIC-IoT-2023 and TON\_IoT, but on CTU-IoT-23 one configuration drives the trend, and none predicts detection consistently on an unseen dataset. Interpretable detectors should be evaluated across several training runs, models and datasets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Internet of Things (IoT) devices make up a large share of network endpoints, and their irregular updates and heterogeneous firmware make them attractive targets for botnet recruitment, DDoS amplification, and command-and-control (C\&C) infrastructure. Because IoT devices cannot run endpoint agents, detection has to work from flows captured at a gateway or router tap~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune}. The attack mix has recognizable signatures: Mirai-family botnets send high-rate UDP and TCP floods~\cite{meidan2018nbaiot}, C\&C heartbeats beacon at regular inter-arrival times, and reconnaissance scans produce asymmetric packet counts. Such signatures lend themselves to rule- and concept-based representations. At the same time, new Mirai sub-families and DDoS variants keep appearing, so a model trained on a handful of attack families will encounter structurally similar but unseen variants in deployment.

Deep-learning NIDS classify known attacks accurately but raise two problems in deployment. The first is \emph{opacity}: without knowing which traffic features drove a decision, operators cannot validate alerts, adjust policies, or meet regulatory requirements~\cite{rudin2019stop,arp2022dos}. Post-hoc explainers such as xNIDS~\cite{wei2023xnids} give more faithful explanations than LIME or SHAP, but they still explain a black box after the fact. The second is the \emph{open-set problem}: a NIDS trained on a closed set of attacks meets new families in deployment~\cite{arp2022dos} and confidently assigns them to known classes, and common remedies such as retraining or a separate anomaly detector do not use the model's own representation for open-set scoring~\cite{han2023owad}.

In this paper, we study both problems together by building interpretability into the architecture and measuring what that does to open-set detection. In \textbf{CBMs}~\cite{koh2020concept}, predictions pass through a layer of human-defined flow concepts that can be inspected and overridden at test time. In \textbf{NeSy-NIDS}, expert attack signatures become threshold rules whose thresholds are learned through $k$-annealed sigmoid gates; a learned weight blends the rule head with a neural fallback, and a binarized rule vector is kept for audit and OOD scoring.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Accuracy without full fidelity.} JointCBM, HybridCBM and NeSy-NIDS match an unconstrained MLP on known classes from source addresses seen in training (SequentialCBM and Post-hoc CBM lose accuracy), but their declared concepts and rules only partly account for their class predictions.
  \item \textbf{Where interpretable representations help detection.} NeSy-NIDS gives the highest CTU OOD AUROC ($+0.030$ over the MLP, 95\% CI $[0.016, 0.043]$; not a higher TPR at 5\% FPR), and concept supervision raises the AUROC of an 8-unit bottleneck on CTU and TON\_IoT, though not on CIC.
  \item \textbf{Geometry is associated with detectability, not a validated predictor.} Concept-fidelity regularisation has no consistent effect on OOD detection; known-class geometry tracks OOD AUROC on two of three corpora, on the third only through one configuration, and with the tested linear model none predicts detectability consistently on an unseen corpus.
  \item \textbf{Scorer selection remains open.} The best OOD scorer depends on dataset and architecture, and neither of two known-traffic selection procedures recovers it on every corpus.
  \item \textbf{Reusable evaluation diagnostics.} Concept replacement, branch agreement, leave-one-configuration-out and leave-one-corpus-out checks, leak-free feature selection and a source-IP holdout, released with code, splits and models.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Scope.} The building blocks, concept bottlenecks~\cite{koh2020concept}, sigmoid-relaxed threshold rules~\cite{petersen2022logic,yang2022ste}, per-class Mahalanobis scoring~\cite{lee2018simple} and neural/rule hybridisation, are established, and we claim no new state-of-the-art score. The contribution is empirical, with evaluation guidance in the spirit of~\cite{arp2022dos}; we make no streaming, concept-drift or false-alert-rate claims. The findings are stated for the models and the concept and rule vocabularies we authored, and, on CTU-IoT-23, for test traffic from source addresses seen in training (Section~\ref{sec:acc}).

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

Post-hoc explanation is the dominant answer to NIDS opacity: xNIDS~\cite{wei2023xnids} uses sparse group lasso to produce human-readable firewall rules, and GEAD~\cite{han2024gead} extracts global rules from a trained deep model. The explanation lives outside the model and can stop matching it when the model changes, so we build interpretability into the architecture instead.

CBMs~\cite{koh2020concept} force predictions through human-specified concepts. Concept embedding models~\cite{zarlenga2022concept} extend concepts to higher-dimensional embeddings, and a trained network can be converted into a CBM post hoc~\cite{yuksekgonul2023posthoc}. Concept bottlenecks are known to leak: Mahinpei et al.~\cite{mahinpei2021promises} show that concept models carry predictive information their labels do not capture. We reproduce this on IoT flows and relate it to the rule-branch dependence of a neuro-symbolic model and to open-set detectability under one multi-seed, multi-dataset protocol. Closest to our setting, Shoukat et al.~\cite{shoukat2026concept} apply a concept bottleneck with prototype reasoning to intrusion detection in SDN-based industrial networks; we differ in evaluating open-set detection on IoT flows, comparing against a neuro-symbolic rule model, and measuring decision fidelity and operating-threshold behaviour.

Petersen et al.~\cite{petersen2022logic} train binary logic-gate networks through smooth relaxations, and Yang et al.~\cite{yang2022ste} inject logical constraints with the straight-through estimator~\cite{bengio2013ste}. NeSy-NIDS applies this line of work to IoT flows, where rules are threshold conjunctions over flow features that an analyst can read and override.

The Mahalanobis detector~\cite{lee2018simple} fits class-conditional Gaussians to learned features, and Fort et al.~\cite{fort2021exploring} found that feature quality matters more for OOD performance than the scoring rule. Softmax-based alternatives include maximum softmax probability~\cite{hendrycks2017baseline} and the energy score~\cite{liu2020energy}. Vaze et al.~\cite{vaze2022openset} report that on visual-recognition benchmarks a better closed-set classifier tends to give better open-set recognition; we test whether this empirical relationship transfers to IoT flows. In security, OWAD~\cite{han2023owad} detects normality shift and CADE~\cite{yang2021cade} isolates drifting samples, both at the system level, whereas we score individual flows. N-BaIoT~\cite{meidan2018nbaiot} and Kitsune~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune} established per-device anomaly detection for IoT but neither classifies attacks nor explains its decisions. Arp et al.~\cite{arp2022dos} and Apruzzese et al.~\cite{apruzzese2022ml} identify closed-world evaluation and opacity as obstacles to deployment, and De Keersmaeker et al.~\cite{dekeersmaeker2023survey} survey the public IoT security datasets.

\subsection{Datasets and Protocol}
\label{sec:datasets}

\textbf{CTU-IoT-23}~\cite{parmisano2020ctu} provides labelled flows from IoT devices captured in a laboratory. Each Zeek conn-log record is joined to per-flow statistics extracted from the packet captures (unmatched records keep them at 0, 1.7\% of flows; PartOfAHorizontalPortScan labels are excluded, Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:features}); we use 31 of the resulting features (28 numeric, 3 categorical), selected on known-class training flows only. \textbf{CIC-IoT-2023}~\cite{neto2023ciciot} covers 33 attack scenarios across 105 devices; we use 39 of its flow features. \textbf{TON\_IoT}~\cite{alsaedi2020toniot} is an independent testbed whose network subset is derived from Zeek logs; we use 23 features from its connection and protocol logs. TON\_IoT enters the CBM scorer and concept-space analyses (Sections~\ref{sec:ood},~\ref{sec:ksweep}) as a third corpus. Its flow logs carry no per-flag counts or inter-arrival times, so three CTU concepts that need them are replaced by connection-state, responsiveness and web-service concepts.

Table~\ref{tab:protocol} summarises the protocol. Known classes are class-balanced by random sampling, and every held-out family is excluded from training and validation. The split and the OOD evaluation subset are the same for all seeds.

The known sets are: CTU \{Benign, C\&C-HeartBeat, DDoS, Okiru\}; CIC \{Benign, DDoS-SYN\_Flood, Mirai-greeth\_flood, Recon-PortScan, VulnerabilityScan\}; TON \{normal, scanning, DDoS, password, injection\}. The held-out families are, for CTU, C\&C, Attack, C\&C-HeartBeat-Attack, C\&C-FileDownload, C\&C-Torii, C\&C-Mirai, C\&C-HeartBeat-FileDownload, FileDownload and Okiru-Attack; for CIC, the dataset's other 29 attack classes (DDoS, DoS, Mirai, reconnaissance, spoofing, web-attack, brute-force and backdoor classes); and for TON, DoS, XSS, backdoor, ransomware and MITM. Each known set is a benign baseline plus one representative family from each dominant malicious category in the dataset (e.g.\ volumetric DDoS, botnet C\&C or Mirai-family traffic, and scanning where the dataset has it; TON adds credential and web attacks), the traffic an operator could plausibly label early in a deployment. Most held-out families are sub-families and protocol variants of these categories, which is the operational open-set case.

\textbf{Preprocessing.} CIC and TON numeric features are standardised with a scaler fit on the training split. CTU features enter in raw units (missing values set to $-1$), with categorical fields integer-encoded from vocabularies built on known-class flows. Concept and rule thresholds were written from documented attack signatures and known-class statistics. No preprocessing statistic or concept or rule threshold uses a held-out family. The submitted CTU list (37 features) was an exception: it was selected by random-forest importance on a sample that included every CTU label, the held-out families among them, which may favour CTU OOD separability (CIC and TON use fixed lists). All CTU results here therefore use the same selection re-run on known-class training rows only (31 features; NeSy-NIDS adds back the two counters its expert rules read), and the submitted list is kept as an ablation. The re-selection leaves the accuracy and open-set findings in place, and it raises rather than lowers the AUROC of the configurations most sensitive to the feature list (Table~\ref{tab:main}, AUROC$^\S$). Every split and evaluation subset is drawn deterministically by the scripts we release.

\textbf{Robustness splits.} We repeat the CBM evaluation on an alternative known set per dataset (CTU \{Benign, DDoS, Okiru, C\&C\}, promoting C\&C to known and demoting C\&C-HeartBeat to unknown; CIC \{Benign, DDoS-ICMP\_Flood, Mirai-udpplain, Recon-PortScan, VulnerabilityScan\}) and on a timestamp-ordered CTU split, in which each known class is ordered by capture time and the earliest 70\% trains and the latest 15\% tests. For CTU, the feature list is re-selected by the same random-forest procedure on each experiment's own known-class training rows, so the family it holds out (and any test-period flow) never informs its features. The alternative split draws its own class-balanced sample, since its known classes differ, while the temporal split reorders the main split's sample by time; a control that uses the main split's 31-feature list on the same sample separates the effect of re-selection from that of the sample, and the accuracy and open-set conclusions hold under both.

\subsection{Concept Bottleneck Models}
\label{sec:cbm}

A CBM~\cite{koh2020concept} factors the classifier into two stages,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{c}} = g(\mathbf{x}),
  \quad\hat{y} = h(\hat{\mathbf{c}}),
\end{equation}
where $g:\mathbb{R}^d\to[0,1]^K$ is the concept encoder (an MLP $d{\to}256{\to}256{\to}64{\to}K$ with LayerNorm and ReLU) and $h:[0,1]^K\to\mathbb{R}^C$ is a linear classifier over the $C$ known classes. We define $K{=}8$ concepts per dataset (e.g.\ \textit{is\_high\_rate}: Rate${>}1000$\,pps; \textit{is\_beaconing}: $1.5{<}$IAT${<}5$\,s $\wedge$ $0.1{<}$Rate${<}5$; \textit{is\_incomplete\_handshake}: syn\_count${>}2$ $\wedge$ fin\_count${=}0$), each a binary label produced by a threshold rule on the raw features (Table~\ref{tab:concepts}). Because the labels are deterministic functions of the input, \emph{concept accuracy} measures how faithfully the bottleneck reproduces these declared predicates, not whether the predicates are semantically correct. $K{=}8$ balances vocabulary against the sample size the shared-covariance Mahalanobis fit needs.

\textbf{Training variants.} \emph{JointCBM} minimises
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}} + \lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}
    + \frac{\gamma}{K}\,\|\hat{\mathbf{c}} - \mathbf{c}_{\text{gt}}\|^2,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{c}_{\text{gt}}\in\{0,1\}^K$ are the concept labels, $\lambda$ weights concept supervision, and $\gamma\in\{0,0.1,0.5,1.0\}$ weights an additional squared-error concept-fidelity term. Both terms push the predicted concepts toward the same labels. \emph{SequentialCBM} trains and freezes $g$ on the concept labels before training $h$. \emph{HybridCBM} concatenates the 64-dimensional encoder embedding with the $K$ concepts as classifier input, so the classifier can use information outside the concept vocabulary. \emph{Post-hoc CBM} fits a logistic-regression probe per concept to the frozen MLP embedding and a logistic-regression classifier to the probe outputs, both on the training split.

\textbf{OOD scoring.} We apply per-class Mahalanobis scoring~\cite{lee2018simple} in concept space. Class means $\boldsymbol{\mu}_c$ and a shared covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ ($\varepsilon{=}10^{-4}$ ridge, pseudoinverse) are fitted to training concept vectors, and the anomaly score is
\begin{equation}
  s(\mathbf{x}) = \min_c\,(\hat{\mathbf{c}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_c)^\top
  \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\hat{\mathbf{c}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_c).
  \label{eq:mahal}
\end{equation}

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{CBM Training and OOD Scoring}
\label{alg:cbm}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\footnotesize
\Require $\{(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{c}_i,y_i)\}$, $\lambda$, $\gamma$
\State Initialise encoder $g$ ($d{\to}256{\to}256{\to}64{\to}K$, LayerNorm+ReLU), linear classifier $h$
\For{each epoch and mini-batch $(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Y})$}
  \State $\hat{\mathbf{C}}{=}g(\mathbf{X})$;\; $\mathcal{L}{=}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(h(\hat{\mathbf{C}}),\mathbf{Y}){+}\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}(\hat{\mathbf{C}},\mathbf{C}){+}\tfrac{\gamma}{K}\|\hat{\mathbf{C}}{-}\mathbf{C}\|^2$;\; update $g,h$
\EndFor
\State $\boldsymbol{\mu}_c{=}\mathrm{mean}\{g(\mathbf{x}_i){:}\,y_i{=}c\}$;\; $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{=}$ regularised shared covariance
\State \textbf{Inference:} $\hat{\mathbf{c}}{=}g(\mathbf{x})$;\; $\hat{y}{=}\arg\max h(\hat{\mathbf{c}})$;\; $s(\mathbf{x})$ by Eq.~\ref{eq:mahal}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{NeSy-NIDS: Differentiable Rule Learning}
\label{sec:nesy}

\textbf{Rule templates.} The $R{=}8$ rule templates per dataset are written from domain knowledge. Each is a conjunction of threshold conditions; representative CTU-IoT-23 rules are \texttt{ddos\_short\_burst} (Rate${>}1000$ $\wedge$ duration${<}0.1$; DDoS), \texttt{okiru\_low\_rate} (Rate${<}500$ $\wedge$ duration${<}2.5$; Okiru) and \texttt{benign\_single\_probe} (orig\_pkts${<}2$ $\wedge$ Rate${<}100$; Benign), and several rules start from concept thresholds (e.g.\ \texttt{cc\_heartbeat\_beaconing} from \textit{is\_beaconing}). The full banks with initial thresholds and declared target classes are in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:rulebank}. Let $\mathcal{I}_r^{>}$ and $\mathcal{I}_r^{<}$ index the greater-than and less-than conditions of rule $r$. Following~\cite{petersen2022logic}, each indicator is replaced by a sigmoid gate and the conjunction by a product:
\begin{equation}
  a_r(\mathbf{x};k) =
    \prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}_r^{>}}\!\sigma\!\bigl(k(x_i{-}\theta_i)\bigr)\cdot
    \prod_{j\in\mathcal{I}_r^{<}}\!\sigma\!\bigl(k(\theta_j{-}x_j)\bigr),
  \label{eq:rule_score}
\end{equation}
so the thresholds $\theta$ receive gradients throughout training.

\textbf{Annealing and binarisation.} The steepness $k$ rises linearly from $k_0{=}1$ to $k_T{=}10$ over $T_{\mathrm{warm}}{=}30$ epochs, $k(t) = k_0 + \min(t/T_{\mathrm{warm}},1)(k_T{-}k_0)$. Training and classification use the soft activations of Eq.~\ref{eq:rule_score}. For audit and OOD scoring, each condition is thresholded and the rule is their logical conjunction,
\begin{equation}
  a_r^{\mathrm{hard}} = \prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}_r^{>}}\mathbf{1}[x_i{>}\theta_i]\;
                        \prod_{j\in\mathcal{I}_r^{<}}\mathbf{1}[x_j{<}\theta_j],
  \label{eq:binarise}
\end{equation}
which is not the same operation as thresholding the soft product.

\textbf{Architecture and rule-head weight.} A linear rule head maps the soft activations, scaled by learned per-rule importance weights, to rule logits $\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{rule}}$. A parallel MLP (hidden layers of 128 and 64 units, LayerNorm and ReLU) on the raw features produces neural logits $\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{neural}}$, and
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{l} = \alpha\,\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{rule}} +
               (1{-}\alpha)\,\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{neural}},\qquad \alpha{=}\sigma(w_\alpha),
  \label{eq:alpha_gate}
\end{equation}
with $w_\alpha$ learned jointly. An optional penalty $\lambda_\alpha(1{-}\alpha)^2$ pushes $\alpha$ upward. $\alpha$ is a single global mixing weight; because the two heads can emit logits of different magnitudes, its value alone does not measure how much the prediction relies on the rules (Section~\ref{sec:fidelity}).

\textbf{OOD scoring in rule space.} The binary vector $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{hard}}(\mathbf{x})\in\{0,1\}^R$ is scored with Eq.~\ref{eq:mahal}, fitted on known-class training activations. Binary vectors are not Gaussian, so this is an approximation; Section~\ref{sec:ood} compares it with binary-native and logit-space scorers.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{NeSy-NIDS Training and OOD Scoring}
\label{alg:nesy}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\footnotesize
\Require $\{(\mathbf{x}_i,y_i)\}$, templates with initial thresholds $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}$, $\lambda_\alpha$, $k_0{=}1$, $k_T{=}10$, $T_{\mathrm{warm}}$
\State Initialise $\boldsymbol{\theta}{=}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}$, rule head $(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{imp}})$, MLP $\phi$, gate $w_\alpha$
\For{epoch $t=1,\ldots,T$}
  \State $k{=}k_0{+}\min(t/T_{\mathrm{warm}},1)(k_T{-}k_0)$
  \For{each mini-batch $(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})$}
    \State $\mathbf{a}{=}\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{X};k)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:rule_score}, soft);\; $\mathbf{l}{=}\alpha\,\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{rule}}(\mathbf{a}){+}(1{-}\alpha)\,\mathrm{MLP}_\phi(\mathbf{X})$, $\alpha{=}\sigma(w_\alpha)$
    \State $\mathcal{L}{=}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{Y}){+}\lambda_\alpha(1{-}\alpha)^2$;\; update $\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{imp}},\phi,w_\alpha$
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State Fit $\boldsymbol{\mu}_c$, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ on $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{hard}}(\mathbf{x}_i)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:binarise}) of the training flows
\State \textbf{Inference:} classify $\hat{y}{=}\arg\max\mathbf{l}$ from soft $\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{x};k_T)$; score $s(\mathbf{x})$ by Eq.~\ref{eq:mahal} on $\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{hard}}(\mathbf{x})$ (OOD and audit)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Open-Set Decision Rule}
\label{sec:openset}

Both classifiers are closed-set, so we couple the anomaly score to the classifier through a rejection threshold $\tau$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y}_{\mathrm{open}}(\mathbf{x}) =
  \begin{cases}
    \textit{unknown}, & s(\mathbf{x}) > \tau,\\[2pt]
    \arg\max_c\, l_c(\mathbf{x}), & \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $l_c$ is the $c$-th class logit. $\tau$ is the 95th percentile of scores on the known-class validation split, so about 5\% of known traffic is rejected; no unknown flow is used to set it.

\subsection{Baselines, Metrics and Training}
\label{sec:eval}

The MLP baseline has the same encoder as the CBM followed by a linear classifier and is scored with Mahalanobis on its 64-dimensional embedding. A decision tree (DT; depth chosen from $\{5,10,15,\infty\}$ by validation F1) is paired with per-class Mahalanobis on the raw training features, and a random forest (RF~\cite{breiman2001random}; 100 trees, depth 10) is scored with $1{-}\max(\hat{p})$. We report weighted F1 on the known test split, and AUROC and TPR at 5\% FPR with known test flows as negatives and held-out unknown flows as positives. All hyperparameters and early stopping use the known-class validation split. Adam ($\mathrm{lr}{=}10^{-3}$, batch 512); CBMs train for up to 50 epochs (patience 10) with $\lambda{=}0.5$, the middle of the grid $\{0.1,0.5,1.0\}$: validation F1 differs by at most $0.0005$ between $\lambda{=}0.1$ and $0.5$, and $\lambda{=}1.0$ is unstable on CTU, where one of five seeds collapses (F1 $0.73$). Raising $\lambda$ from $0.1$ to $1.0$ raises concept accuracy (CTU $0.70{\to}0.94$, CIC $0.87{\to}0.98$) but moves OOD AUROC in opposite directions (CTU $0.810{\to}0.830$, CIC $0.743{\to}0.703$; five seeds, Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:lambdagamma}); NeSy-NIDS trains for up to 60 epochs (patience 12) with $\lambda_\alpha\in\{0,0.1\}$.

\textbf{Evaluation subsets.} All models share the training, validation and test splits of their corpus. Every row of Tables~\ref{tab:main}, \ref{tab:scorer} and~\ref{tab:ood_baselines}, and the NeSy-NIDS $\lambda_\alpha$ and $k_T$ sweeps, are scored on one fixed set of flows per corpus: $20{,}000$ training flows fit each scorer, AUROC compares $5{,}000$ known test flows with $5{,}000$ flows of the unknown draw (Table~\ref{tab:protocol}), and TPR sets its threshold at the 95th percentile over all known test flows and is measured on the whole unknown draw; both sets are the same for every model. This TPR is a test-ROC point; the open-set decision uses the validation threshold $\tau$ (Section~\ref{sec:openset}). The scorer\,$\times$\,architecture and scorer-selection analyses draw a separate subset per corpus, so a model can differ slightly between tables (CTU MLP $0.874$ vs.\ $0.879$); we compare values only within one analysis.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Classification Accuracy}
\label{sec:acc}

\begin{sloppypar}\hbadness=10000
Table~\ref{tab:main} shows NeSy-NIDS and every JointCBM variant tracking the MLP on both datasets. Paired per-seed F1 differences from the MLP have means of at most $0.0001$ in magnitude on CTU-IoT-23 (no seed beyond $0.0004$) and $-0.0004$ to $+0.0002$ on CIC-IoT-2023 (no seed beyond $0.0026$). These differences are well inside seed variation; we do not test statistical equivalence. For JointCBM ($\gamma{=}0$) the match also holds per class: every class is within $0.002$ of the MLP. The weighted F1 hides a spread across classes: the weakest are CTU Okiru ($0.86$) and DDoS ($0.90$), and CIC Recon-PortScan ($0.64$), VulnerabilityScan ($0.70$) and Benign ($0.76$).
\end{sloppypar}

The rule-only head reaches only $0.566$ on CTU-IoT-23 and $0.750$ on CIC-IoT-2023, so the neural fallback covers traffic the eight rule templates do not. SequentialCBM and Post-hoc CBM lose much of their accuracy (CTU $0.567$/$0.576$; CIC $0.732$/$0.746$): consistent with a linear classifier over separately learned concepts being unable to recover the class boundary. The $\gamma$ variants have near-identical F1 in the main runs; in the supplementary $(\lambda,\gamma)$ grid two of the fifteen CTU runs at $\lambda{=}1.0$ collapse (F1 $0.73$--$0.74$).

\textbf{Robustness.} On the alternative known sets the MLP reaches F1 $0.935$ (CTU) and $0.820$ (CIC), and every JointCBM variant matches it within $0.001$ on both. The timestamp-ordered split lowers four-class weighted F1 from $0.933$ to $0.646$--$0.648$ for every model in Table~\ref{tab:temporal} except SequentialCBM ($0.330$). The CTU \emph{DDoS} label spans two capture regimes (a high-rate flood, then a near-zero-rate capture), so the temporal split trains on one and tests on the other, and every model scores F1 $0.00$ on DDoS on every seed; on the same sample with the main split's 31-feature list DDoS also collapses for every model and seed except two JointCBM $\gamma{=}0.5$ seeds, so the cause is the label's temporal structure, not feature selection. On the three temporally-coherent families the interpretable models match the MLP in aggregate (Coh-3 $0.862$--$0.863$) while trading per-class strengths (JointCBM $\gamma{=}0$: Benign $0.841$ vs.\ $0.958$, Okiru $0.961$ vs.\ $0.844$); only SequentialCBM, which loses Benign, falls behind. The accuracy match is therefore a matched-distribution result.

\textbf{Source-IP holdout.} Random flow splits place flows from the same source addresses in training and test, so we also hold out source IP addresses on two corpora (five seeds; the MLP, the CBM variants and NeSy-NIDS, the last on CTU only). Our merged flow tables keep the source IP but not the capture name, and an address can recur across captures, so a held-out address is not shown to be one physical device: the test separates source addresses. Three CTU addresses (192.168.1.198, 192.168.100.111, 192.168.100.108) have enough flows in every known class to form the test set while all other addresses train and validate. On TON\_IoT the attack flows of each known class come from a few source addresses, so we hold out 192.168.1.30 or .31 for the attack classes and one address for normal traffic. To test open-set detection on a held-out address, we also score its known flows against its own unknown-family flows (MLP, CBMs and NeSy-NIDS; address .198: mostly C\&C; TON\_IoT: DoS). On TON\_IoT part of the classification performance and AUROC is retained (MLP weighted F1 $0.876$ on the main split, $0.761$ and $0.886$ on addresses .30 and .31; DoS AUROC $0.41$--$0.75$ on address .30, $0.44$--$0.75$ on seen addresses), but TPR at 5\% FPR is at most $0.7\%$ (.30) and $2.6\%$ (.31), and ${\le}2.2\%$ on seen addresses. On CTU, whose held-out addresses produce class traffic unlike that of the other addresses (address .198's $3{,}450$ Benign test flows are three distinct feature vectors absent from the Benign training flows), classification fails on all three for every model (mean F1 $0.42$, $0.14$--$0.23$ and $0.34$--$0.55$, against $0.57$--$0.93$ on the main split), and C\&C AUROC falls from $0.82$--$0.88$ to $0.40$--$0.71$. With $\tau$ set on validation traffic from the training addresses, the known rejection rate also departs from its 5\% target on held-out addresses (up to $50\%$ on address .198, $4$--$13\%$ on TON\_IoT), while unknown recall stays at most $13\%$ (Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:device}). The CTU results therefore hold for source addresses seen in training; without the capture identifier we did not build a capture-disjoint split.

\subsection{Open-Set Detection}
\label{sec:ood}

\textbf{CTU-IoT-23.} Under the leak-free feature selection the highest observed means come from NeSy-NIDS with the $\alpha$ penalty ($0.909{\pm}.014$) and NeSy-NIDS ($0.904{\pm}.014$), followed by the MLP ($0.874{\pm}.002$), SequentialCBM ($0.872{\pm}.026$) and Post-hoc CBM ($0.871{\pm}.024$); NeSy-NIDS exceeds the MLP by $0.030$ (95\% CI $[0.016, 0.043]$; bootstrap over seeds and evaluation flows under these splits, Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:ci}); its mean TPR at 5\% FPR ($0.493$) is not higher than the MLP's ($0.519$). The four JointCBM variants reach $0.81$--$0.84$ (seed std $0.04$--$0.05$), HybridCBM $0.822$, and the DT and RF baselines $0.679$ and $0.657$. SequentialCBM, whose concept head is trained on the concept labels alone and frozen, has an observed AUROC similar to the MLP's (difference $-0.002$, CI $[-0.027, 0.018]$) despite a closed-set F1 of only $0.567$. With the originally submitted feature list on the same rows, SequentialCBM and HybridCBM reach only $0.782$ and $0.772$ and Post-hoc CBM $0.805$, so known-class-only selection raises them, while the MLP moves from $0.894$ to $0.874$ and NeSy-NIDS from $0.885$ to $0.904$ (Table~\ref{tab:main}, AUROC$^\S$). NeSy-NIDS also reads the two rule counters that the re-selection drops (Section~\ref{sec:datasets}); given the same 33 columns, the MLP reaches $0.878{\pm}.001$, JointCBM $\gamma{=}0$ $0.804{\pm}.021$, SequentialCBM $0.852{\pm}.038$ and HybridCBM $0.790{\pm}.069$, so the two counters do not account for NeSy-NIDS's higher mean.

\textbf{CIC-IoT-2023.} The MLP ($0.807{\pm}.007$) and the DT's raw-feature Mahalanobis ($0.802$) have the highest AUROC. The JointCBM variants reach $0.699$--$0.717$; SequentialCBM and HybridCBM reach $0.667$ and $0.669$, and NeSy-NIDS with Mahalanobis only $0.647$ ($0.672$ with the $\alpha$ penalty). Unlike on CTU, SequentialCBM is among the weakest detectors here. The low-dimensional concept and rule spaces separate these unknowns less well than the 64-dimensional MLP embedding and 39 raw features. A dimension-matched control, the JointCBM architecture trained with class labels but without concept supervision ($\lambda{=}\gamma{=}0$, an 8-unit bottleneck; five seeds), reaches $0.719{\pm}.023$, close to JointCBM (difference $+0.003$, CI $[-0.024, 0.027]$), which is consistent with the CIC gap to the MLP arising mainly from the 8-unit width rather than from the concepts. On CTU the same control is unstable ($0.561{\pm}.154$; seeds $0.44$--$0.86$, F1 $0.867$) and concept supervision recovers most of the loss (JointCBM $0.823$); on TON\_IoT it reaches $0.544{\pm}.115$ against $0.650{\pm}.044$ for JointCBM and $0.552{\pm}.027$ for the MLP.

\textbf{Concept-fidelity regularisation is not a lever.} The sign of $\Delta{=}\gamma_{1.0}{-}\gamma_0$ is opposite on the two main splits, and across five dataset/split combinations it is positive on two and negative on three, with $|\Delta|\le0.06$ (Table~\ref{tab:gamma_splits}); on both main splits the 95\% CI of $\Delta$ includes zero (CTU $[-0.052, 0.065]$, CIC $[-0.037, 0.002]$), so $\gamma$ gives no consistent improvement. In a joint $(\lambda,\gamma)$ grid (five seeds per cell; Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:lambdagamma}) the size of the $\gamma$ effect also changes with $\lambda$: on CTU it grows from $-0.004$ at $\lambda{=}0.1$ to $+0.030$ at $\lambda{=}1.0$, and on CIC it shrinks from $-0.040$ to $-0.003$. Effects this small need multi-seed replication under a fixed scoring protocol.

\textbf{Scorer selection.} We tested two procedures for choosing the scorer without the novel traffic. The first holds out one known attack family, retrains JointCBM ($\gamma{=}0$) on the rest, and selects the scorer (Mahalanobis or energy) that better detects the held-out family (five seeds per family; Table~\ref{tab:lofo}). On CTU the family-majority vote recovers the true-best Mahalanobis (two of three families, $9/15$ folds). On CIC it picks Mahalanobis for all four families ($15/20$ folds) although energy is best on the true unknowns: the held-out known families stay separable in activation space while the true unknowns do not, so the proxy is confidently wrong on the corpus where the scorer must switch. The second needs no retraining: it builds synthetic outliers by permuting each feature independently across known validation flows and selects the scorer that better separates them from other validation flows. Against the better scorer on the true unknowns (Table~\ref{tab:scorer_arch}), it is right for $25/35$ CTU and $26/35$ CIC models, recovering energy for most CIC JointCBM models, but for only $9/35$ TON\_IoT models, where it keeps choosing Mahalanobis; overall ($60/105$) it does no better than always choosing Mahalanobis ($62/105$).

\subsection{Fidelity of the Concept and Rule Representations}
\label{sec:fidelity}

Readable concepts or rules need not account for the predictions made from them, so we test fidelity separately from accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:fidelity}). \textbf{CBM.} We separate the soft predicted concepts, their thresholded values, and the declared predicates used as labels. A linear probe on the soft predicted concepts gains little over one on their thresholded values on CTU-IoT-23 (except for two $\gamma{=}1.0$ seeds) and more on CIC-IoT-2023, where the gain grows rather than shrinks with $\gamma$ (Table~\ref{tab:fidelity}). A small gain does not establish fidelity, because thresholded predictions can disagree with the declared predicates and still carry class information. We therefore also pass the declared predicates through the model's own classifier, and train a logistic regression on them directly. Both fall far below the learned concepts (on CTU the replacement F1 has seed std $0.02$--$0.04$), so under the evaluated linear classifiers the declared predicates do not reproduce the model's accuracy. Replacement also changes the classifier's input distribution, so these drops test decision fidelity, not a decomposition of accuracy by source. Raising $\gamma$ improves concept-label agreement on CTU ($87.4\%$ to $94.2\%$; Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:leakage}) but leaves the gap within seed variability.

\subsection{Known-Class Geometry: Consistent on Two Corpora, Configuration-Driven on CTU}
\label{sec:ksweep}

Across the ten NeSy-NIDS models, mean within-class rule-activation variance tracks OOD AUROC ($r{=}0.973$), but the models form two dataset clusters, so this correlation cannot separate a property of the representation from a property of the dataset. We therefore test known-class statistics across the thirty CBM concept-space models per dataset (six configurations $\times$ five seeds) on three corpora. On TON\_IoT the JointCBM variants reach known-class F1 $0.879$--$0.898$ (MLP $0.876$) and concept-space AUROC $0.65$, above the MLP embedding's $0.55$.

\textbf{Within-class variance.} On CIC-IoT-2023 and TON\_IoT more within-class variance goes with worse detection ($r{=}-0.77$ and $-0.46$). On CTU-IoT-23 the slope is weakly \emph{positive} ($+0.24$) and comes from SequentialCBM, which shares the highest within-class variance with HybridCBM but is also the strongest CTU concept-space detector ($0.872$, against $0.822$ for HybridCBM; Fig.~\ref{fig:confounding}). Without SequentialCBM it is $+0.01$ (Table~\ref{tab:signflip}). The within-dataset direction also does not carry across corpora: the three dataset means are non-monotone ($r{=}+0.36$ over three points), so a corpus's mean known-class variance does not rank its detectability.

\textbf{Seven statistics.} We tested seven statistics computed from known classes only: within-class variance, Fisher trace ratio $\mathrm{tr}(S_B)/\mathrm{tr}(S_W)$, ridge-LDA separability $\mathrm{tr}((S_W{+}\varepsilon I)^{-1}S_B)$, silhouette, nearest-centroid margin, concept-activation entropy, and closed-set F1. On CIC-IoT-2023 and TON\_IoT each keeps the same sign (Table~\ref{tab:signflip}): more known-class separability, or less within-class variance, goes with higher OOD AUROC, as intuition and Vaze et al.~\cite{vaze2022openset} predict. On CTU-IoT-23 all seven take the opposite sign ($|r|\le0.37$). Without SequentialCBM, which pairs the lowest closed-set F1 and the least separable concept space with one of the highest CTU AUROCs, they shrink to $|r|\le0.22$ and three change sign (Table~\ref{tab:signflip}, CTU$^\ast$), so the CTU reversal is a property of one configuration rather than of the corpus. Correlations pooled over configurations therefore need such a leave-one-configuration-out check before they are read as a property of a dataset. These are within-corpus associations, and they do not transfer consistently: fitting AUROC on each statistic over two corpora and predicting the held-out third, the prediction beats the training-corpora mean AUROC in only $3$ of $21$ corpus--statistic cases, ranks the held-out models in the right direction in $7$ of $21$, and identifies the best configuration in $1$ of $21$.

\textbf{A manipulation and an interpretation.} Retraining NeSy-NIDS at annealing steepness $k_T\in\{2,5,10,20\}$ (five seeds, constant known-class F1) raises within-class variance on CIC ($0.024{\to}0.037$) but AUROC follows only weakly ($r{=}0.44$, $0.61$ at $k_T{\le}5$ vs.\ $0.65$ at $k_T{\ge}10$); on CTU variance barely moves ($0.125$--$0.128$) and is not positively associated with AUROC ($r{=}-0.15$). This is consistent with a known-class-conditional scorer depending on where the unknown flows fall, not on the known classes alone; we state the result only for the settings tested.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Deployment implications.} The threshold $\tau$ needs only known validation traffic and held the known rejection rate near its target on the main splits (Section~\ref{sec:ood}), though not on held-out source addresses (Section~\ref{sec:acc}); neither tested scorer-selection procedure was reliable (limitation~(iii)). $\gamma$ offers no reliable OOD benefit, so effort is better spent matching the scorer to the representation and reading the concept or rule vector within the fidelity limits of Section~\ref{sec:fidelity}.

\phantomsection\label{sec:compute}
\textbf{Computational cost.} On CTU-IoT-23, JointCBM adds 296 parameters to the MLP and NeSy-NIDS has 13{,}313, all under $0.4$\,MB in \texttt{float32}. Per-flow (batch-1) latency on one thread of an Intel Core i9-9820X is $96$\,$\mu$s (MLP), $109$\,$\mu$s (JointCBM) and about $590$\,$\mu$s (NeSy-NIDS, an un-vectorised per-rule loop), measured with the submitted 37-feature input.

\textbf{Limitations and next steps.} (i)~\emph{Authored vocabulary.} The concepts and rules were written by us from documented attack signatures~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune,meidan2018nbaiot} and not validated by operators or independent experts; an operator study of the vocabulary is the next step. (ii)~\emph{Source-address shift and sampling.} The main splits are random at the flow level, and our holdout separates source IP addresses, not verified physical devices; on TON\_IoT, classification performance and AUROC are partly retained under source-IP holdout, but unknown-attack recall remains low and the known-traffic rejection rate can exceed its calibration target, while on CTU the learned models fail to classify held-out addresses (Section~\ref{sec:acc}). We built no capture-disjoint split (our merged CTU table lacks the capture identifier), and each split is a single class-balanced sample; corpora with many devices per attack class and multi-sample evaluation are the next steps. (iii)~\emph{Scorer selection.} Neither selection rule we tested picks the better scorer on every corpus (Section~\ref{sec:ood}); richer pseudo-unknowns are the next step. (iv)~\emph{Geometry as a predictor.} No known-class statistic transfers consistently across corpora with the tested linear model (Section~\ref{sec:ksweep}); more corpora are needed to test any corpus-level property. (v)~\emph{Timing} was measured only on a desktop CPU, not on gateway hardware. (vi)~\emph{Coverage.} NeSy-NIDS and the temporal, alternative and source-IP splits cover one or two datasets, the geometry three. All corpora are fixed-vector flow representations with hand-authored concepts; packet-sequence, graph and learned-concept representations remain untested.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

With our authored concept and rule vocabularies, JointCBM, HybridCBM and NeSy-NIDS match an unconstrained MLP on known classes from source addresses seen in training, whereas SequentialCBM and Post-hoc CBM lose accuracy; the representations can aid detection: NeSy-NIDS gives the highest CTU AUROC, and concept supervision improves an 8-unit bottleneck on two of three corpora. Their concepts and rules, however, only partly account for their class predictions, open-set detection depends on dataset, architecture and scorer, and at a usable threshold they miss nearly all flows of several unfamiliar families. Known-class statistics track detectability on two corpora, on the third only through one configuration, and with the tested linear model none predicts it consistently on an unseen corpus. Evaluations of interpretable NIDS should report decision fidelity and per-family recall alongside accuracy, use leak-free feature selection, support open-set claims with multiple seeds, architectures, datasets and held-out source addresses, and check pooled correlations with a leave-one-configuration-out analysis. The next steps are corpora with many devices per class and a scorer-selection rule that holds on every corpus.
